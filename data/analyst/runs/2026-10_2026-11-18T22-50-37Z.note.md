The augmentation reading holds this month: AI-exposed work is being reshuffled, not eliminated, based on the available data. That call rests on the absence of deterioration in the numbers that would show workers failing to find new jobs, not on direct proof that AI is making anyone more productive.

The only panel that moved since last month was job postings. Postings for AI-exposed, knowledge-work roles sank further below their pre-pandemic level while postings for hands-on control-industry roles (construction, retail, personal services) held roughly steady above theirs. This gap has now widened for several months running without flattening out.

The strongest case for the reading: the employment rate for prime-age workers, the number that would catch displaced workers failing to land elsewhere, has been flat over the past year and sits above its pre-pandemic level, and layoffs are low and still falling. That is not what a labor market shedding workers looks like.

The strongest case against it, and the part that should not be read past: buried inside that same calm employment data, the share of unemployed workers who have been out of work long-term has risen about three and a half points over the past year, and that rise has been accelerating, picking up roughly two points in just the last quarter. That is exactly the kind of early warning that would show up before the headline number turns, if re-employment were starting to fail. It has not reversed. Separately, the job-postings gap itself cannot tell you whether firms are posting fewer AI-exposed jobs because existing workers now cover more output with AI tools, or because firms are quietly pulling back ahead of cuts the employment data hasn't caught yet.

What to watch next: the job postings gap between AI-exposed and control occupations, since it is the freshest-moving number here and the one most likely to turn first if this reading is wrong.
