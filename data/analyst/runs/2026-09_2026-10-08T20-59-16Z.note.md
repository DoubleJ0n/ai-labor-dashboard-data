The call is augmentation: AI-exposed industries are shedding workers faster than the rest of the economy, but those workers appear to be finding other jobs rather than going unemployed. That reading rests on an absence of deterioration, not on proof that AI is making anyone more productive, and nothing changed in the underlying data since the previous check two days ago — every panel holds the same value.

The strongest support: the prime-age employment rate (ages 25-54), the best gauge of whether displaced workers are landing elsewhere, has held flat over the past year and sits above its 2019 level, while hiring and layoffs are both moving in a healthy direction. Control industries outside the AI-exposed group are still adding jobs at 1.19% a year, not a recessionary pace. Together these say the labor market is absorbing the outflow from AI-exposed industries rather than stranding it.

The strongest thing against it: job postings in AI-exposed occupations remain 21.8% below their pre-pandemic level, against postings in non-exposed occupations sitting 8.2% above theirs, and that 30-point gap has barely moved in six months even as AI use among firms climbed from 17.3% to 23.8% over the same stretch. A gap that size holding still, in exactly the occupations AI targets, is as consistent with suppressed demand for this work as with reallocation, and it is one of two live warning signs here, alongside a rising share of long-term unemployment that is the one part of the jobs-landing picture not improving. Separately, AI-exposed industries show faster pay growth than other industries (4.12% versus 3.40%), but because employment in those industries is shrinking at the same time, that could reflect senior staff staying while junior staff leave rather than genuine wage gains, so it is not being treated as confirming evidence.

What to watch: the job postings gap between AI-exposed and other occupations, which is the indicator most likely to move first if this reading is turning.
