The verdict is displacement, unchanged from last month, held at low confidence — and this month, the core data behind it didn't move at all.

What changed: nothing material. Two economy-wide, descriptive figures nudged slightly (the decades-long decline in labor's income share eased a touch; the yield curve steepened slightly), but neither is a panel the verdict rests on.

The strongest evidence for displacement: employment in AI-exposed industries fell 1.47% over the past year while employment in less-exposed control industries (construction, education, health, leisure) rose 1.4%, a gap that has widened for four straight months. That alone could just mean workers moving to other sectors. What tips it toward displacement is that the broader prime-age employment rate — the measure that would catch people whether they land in another industry or drop out of work entirely — also turned down over the past year, crossing this dashboard's line for workers not being reabsorbed.

The strongest evidence against: inside that same data, actual hiring behavior looks calm rather than distressed — hiring relative to quits is improving and layoffs aren't elevated — and the freshest, most forward-looking indicator, the gap between postings for knowledge work versus hands-on work, has sat flat for six months instead of widening. Economy-wide wage growth for job-switchers also remains positive. None of this proves nothing is happening, but it means the case for displacement rests on one measure crossing a line while several adjacent measures stay calm, which is why confidence is low rather than firm.

What to watch: the prime-age employment ratio in the reabsorption panel, the next reading of which will show whether that downturn deepens or reverses.
