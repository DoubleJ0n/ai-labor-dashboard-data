The reading this month is augmentation: AI-exposed industries are losing jobs faster than other industries, but the broader labor market is still absorbing those losses rather than stockpiling unemployed workers. This verdict rests on the absence of deterioration, not on positive proof that AI is making exposed workers more valuable, and that distinction matters for how much weight to put on it.

What changed since last month: almost nothing. Of the panels on the dashboard, only one moved at all. Job postings in AI-exposed industries (information, professional and business services, finance) sit 21.8% below their pre-pandemic level, while postings in less-exposed industries (construction, hospitality, education and health) sit 8.2% above theirs. That gap widened slightly this month, but the underlying pattern has been essentially flat for six months, and the bulk of the gap predates current AI adoption, tracing back to a 2022 pullback in white-collar hiring when interest rates rose. This is not a fresh signal; it is a stale one. Every other panel held at its prior reading.

The strongest evidence behind the augmentation call is the prime-age employment picture. The share of 25-to-54-year-olds who are employed has not moved at all over the past 12 months, and sits 0.75 points above where it stood before the pandemic. Hiring and quitting in the job-openings data continue at healthy rates, and layoffs are still falling, not rising. If AI were pushing displaced workers out of the labor force altogether, this is the kind of chart where it would show up first, and it is not showing up.

The strongest evidence against it is the direct comparison between AI-exposed and other industries. Employment in exposed industries fell 1.6% over the past year while employment in other industries rose 1.19%, a gap that has now sat outside its normal historical range for two straight months without narrowing. That is exactly what you would expect to see early in a displacement story, and the only thing keeping this reading from tipping toward displacement is that the prime-age employment figure above has not moved with it. There is a second piece of evidence worth naming at full strength: the share of unemployed workers who have been out of work long-term has risen 3.5 percentage points over the past year. It has not yet crossed the level the dashboard treats as a warning line, but it is the one indicator moving in the wrong direction while everything else holds steady, and it is consistent with a scenario where displaced workers are quietly leaving the labor force rather than showing up as unemployed, which would let the flat employment-ratio number mask a real problem building underneath it.

Other context: wages in exposed industries are growing slightly faster than in other industries, but this is ambiguous on its own, because industries that are shedding workers overall will show faster average pay growth even with no genuine wage gain, simply because the departing workers tend to be more junior. A separate, individual-level wage series that is not distorted by this effect shows job-switchers earning a modest premium over people who stay put, which does not support a severe wage story but is also economy-wide rather than specific to exposed industries, so it cannot settle the question either way. Productivity growth, also economy-wide, cooled to 2.2% from 2.9% the prior quarter, still above its long-run average but no longer in the elevated range it had been running in. AI adoption among firms continues to climb, now 23.8%, concentrated more heavily among larger employers, which means an AI-driven explanation for the exposed-industry weakness remains available even though no direct link between any employer's AI use and its layoffs has been demonstrated.

Taken together, nothing this month closes the case either way. The gap between exposed and other industries' job counts remains wide and is not healing, which keeps a displacement story alive and credible. The reason the verdict still lands on augmentation is that the one measure built to catch workers actually being pushed out of the labor force, the prime-age employment ratio, has not moved. That is a thinner basis for confidence than a clearly improving picture would be, and the long-term unemployment share creeping upward is the specific crack to watch.
