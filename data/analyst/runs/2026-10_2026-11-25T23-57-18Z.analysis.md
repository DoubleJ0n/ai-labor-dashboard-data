The reading this month is augmentation, unchanged from last month, and it rests on an absence of bad news rather than on proof that AI is making workers more productive. That distinction matters because it is easy to collapse the two: "no sign of mass layoffs yet" is not the same claim as "AI is clearly helping," and this note is only making the first one.

What changed since last month: almost nothing. Two panels ticked slightly, and neither is one the verdict depends on. Workers' share of national income slipped a tenth of a point further (from -1 to -1.1 on its own index, continuing a slow multi-decade decline that predates AI and cannot be attributed to it). The macro backdrop eased slightly too, with long-term interest rate signals drifting marginally toward easier conditions. Every panel this verdict actually rests on, prime-age employment, the exposed-versus-control jobs gap, the postings spread, is reading exactly where it was a month ago.

The strongest evidence behind the augmentation reading is the prime-age employment-population ratio, the main gauge of whether people who lose work are finding new work. Over the past year it hasn't moved at all, and this flatness sits comfortably inside its normal historical range rather than near any level that would signal trouble. Layoffs, separately, are running below their usual pace, and the share of unemployed people who are underemployed (working part-time but wanting full-time hours) is roughly flat. Put together, this is a frozen-but-not-breaking labor market: hiring has slowed, but people already in jobs are not being pushed out of them in unusual numbers. That is the "displacement hasn't started" case, and it is a real, positive finding, not merely a non-finding.

Against that: AI-exposed industries (professional and business services, finance, information) are hiring noticeably worse than non-exposed industries, exposed-sector employment is actually shrinking year over year while the non-exposed control industries are still growing. That gap has now sat past the level the dashboard treats as the line distinguishing ordinary reallocation from attributable divergence, for three straight months. It is worth testing whether this is really AI-specific or just a broader slowdown: construction, the control-side industry most sensitive to high interest rates, is still growing at a solid pace, which argues against a simple "the whole economy is cooling" explanation and keeps something exposed-industry-specific in the frame.

The single most important piece of evidence cutting against the augmentation reading, and the reason this call is held with low rather than higher confidence, is job postings. Looking at postings by occupation (a sharper way to measure AI exposure than looking at whole industries), postings for knowledge-type work have kept losing ground relative to postings for hands-on work, continuing to widen over the past six months, and this is the freshest data on the whole dashboard. Postings are a leading indicator: they are the kind of series that would be expected to move before the employment and reabsorption numbers show any damage. It has moved the wrong way for six straight months and has not reversed. This has not yet shown up as actual job losses or stalled reabsorption, but it is the live, unresolved warning sign sitting underneath an otherwise calm headline.

Two more things worth naming plainly. First, pay in AI-exposed industries can't currently be used to settle anything: the pay gap between exposed and non-exposed work nearly vanished this month, but because exposed-industry employment is shrinking, a falling headcount can produce the same pay pattern whether AI is paying remaining workers more or whether it's simply the lower-paid workers who left first. The data can't tell those two stories apart right now. Second, the finding that people who switch jobs are still earning a slightly larger pay premium than people who stay put is economy-wide, not specific to AI-exposed work, and it only describes people who successfully found a new job. It says nothing about anyone who tried to switch out of exposed work and could not, or who left the labor force altogether; that population is invisible to this measure, so it should be read as mild reassurance for the people it covers, not a clean bill of health for exposed workers generally.

No single named alternative explanation (a general economic slowdown, or a healthcare-specific hiring boom propping up the "control" industries) is confirmed by this month's data, and both remain live, untested possibilities rather than settled causes.
