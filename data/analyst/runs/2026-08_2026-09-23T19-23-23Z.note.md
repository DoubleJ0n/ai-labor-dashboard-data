The reading stays at displacement: AI-exposed industries are losing jobs in a way the rest of the economy is not, and the broader measure of whether displaced workers find other work has turned down past its warning line. This is not a call resting on the absence of bad news — both of those movements are real and currently in place.

What's new since last month: almost nothing. Every panel that actually drives this verdict — the jobs and wage gap between exposed and non-exposed industries, the prime-age employment rate, worker income share, AI adoption, the macro backdrop — reads exactly as it did a month ago. The only two things that moved were minor: the gap between job postings in AI-exposed occupations and hands-on occupations widened slightly (28.8 percentage points versus 27.9 last month), and a capability benchmark used only as background context ticked down. Neither changes the picture.

The strongest evidence for displacement: employment in AI-exposed industries fell 1.47% over the past year while employment in comparable non-exposed industries (construction, education, healthcare) rose 1.4%. Because the non-exposed side is healthy, this doesn't look like a general economic slowdown, it looks specific to the industries AI is expected to affect most. Separately, and economy-wide rather than limited to those industries, the share of prime-age adults who are employed has fallen 0.3 percentage point over the past year, enough to cross the level the dashboard treats as real cooling in re-employment.

The strongest evidence complicating this: the way exposed industries are shrinking looks like a freeze, not a flood. Layoffs there are currently running below normal, and hiring versus quitting is flat, not the pattern of workers being actively pushed out. That points to employers simply not adding or replacing jobs rather than shedding staff, a slower and quieter mechanism than outright displacement. And the earliest-moving indicator here, the postings gap between AI-exposed and hands-on occupations, has been flat for six months rather than widening further, when it is exactly the panel that should move first if displacement were intensifying. It hasn't. Confidence in this reading stays low.

What to watch: the prime-age employment-population ratio in the reabsorption panel, whose 12-month change would need to turn back toward flat or better for this reading to reverse.
