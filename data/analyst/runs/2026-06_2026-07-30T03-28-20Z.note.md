# Published note 2026-06 (run 2026-07-30T03:28:20Z)

Verdict: DISPLACEMENT | 705 words (extended (a month with something to explain)) | 17 numbers
Counts are reported, not enforced: length is proportional to what changed and the number ceiling is retired.
Notification: Displacement holds on a flat month; new job-switcher pay data is the strongest counter.
Falsifier: If the prime-age employment-rate chart in the reabsorption panel climbs back so its 12-month change is no worse than -0.1 while the long-term-unemployed share falls back to 26% or below, people are landing again and this displacement reading was wrong.

---

The reading is displacement, and it rests on something happening rather than on the absence of good news: jobs in AI-exposed industries are actually shrinking, by 1.27% over the past year, while jobs in the control industries AI has barely touched grew 1.27%, and the people coming out are not landing again.

Nothing moved this month. The jobs gap between exposed and control industries shifted by four hundredths of a point, pay growth on each side by a hundredth, the share of firms using AI not at all. The household and payroll figures still run through June, so every labour reading is the one from last month. The reading rests on levels that were already there, not on fresh deterioration, and that is worth saying plainly rather than dressing up a flat month.

The one new thing is a measure that follows the same individuals over time, and it argues against displacement — see below.

What makes this displacement rather than workers reshuffling between industries is the absorption side. The share of 25-to-54-year-olds with a job has fallen half a percentage point over twelve months, in a series that normally rises about four-tenths of a point a year, and prime-age employment itself is down about 1.4 million people, so this is not an artefact of a growing population. The share of unemployed people out of work six months or longer has climbed four points to 27.3%. If only the exposed industries were weak, that would be reallocation. People staying unemployed longer is what turns it into displacement.

Three things cut the other way and one of them is new.

Postings for knowledge work sit about 23% below their February 2020 level while postings for hands-on work sit about 4% above theirs — but that gap has been closing, by 3.9 points over six months and nine-tenths in the last month. Closing is what recovery looks like. This is the freshest data on the board, a month ahead of everything supporting the displacement reading, and it points the other way. One caveat readers deserve: the published figures show the change in the gap but not in each side separately, so it is impossible to tell whether knowledge-work postings are recovering or hands-on postings are falling back. If it is the second, the narrowing is not good news at all.

The new individual-level pay data shows people who change jobs still getting about eight-tenths of a point more wage growth than people who stay, above the pre-2020 norm of six-tenths and recovering. That is people moving because they see something better, not because they were pushed. It covers the whole workforce, including exposed workers, so it cannot be waved away as measuring the wrong people. It is a median, though, so a collapse concentrated in a minority is exactly what it would miss.

And layoffs are entirely normal, at 1.1% of employment, while hiring is weak. That combination is not an economy shedding workers, it is one that has stopped taking people in. A frozen market has a perfectly good explanation in interest rates, and its burden falls mainly on people entering work, who are mostly under 25 and mostly outside the prime-age figures above.

There is also a real residual to be honest about. Information and professional services overhired badly in 2021 and 2022 and have been unwinding since, and that explains why these gaps opened. It does not explain why they are still here. An unwind decays; four years on, exposed employment is not just below trend but contracting outright, while the controls — which also overhired, in leisure and hospitality — are growing. That leftover is what this reading is built on, and it is a thinner thing than the headline gap suggests.

Two figures that get quoted as evidence do not belong here. Productivity growth at 2.8% a year and the worker share of national income at a record-low 51.0% both cover the whole economy, not AI-exposed work, and output per hour rises mechanically whenever hours are cut faster than output falls. Neither tells augmentation and displacement apart.

What to watch: the prime-age employment rate in the reabsorption panel. If it turns back up, people are landing again and this reading is wrong.
