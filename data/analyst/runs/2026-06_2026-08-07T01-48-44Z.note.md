---

The reading is still displacement, held with low confidence, and it rests on positive evidence of deterioration rather than on the absence of good news: employment among 25-to-54-year-olds is down about 1.44 million over twelve months, and the share of unemployed people who have been out of work more than six months has risen 4.0 points to 27.3%.

Nothing in the labor data moved this month. Jobs, the employment rate, pay, postings and productivity all carry the same readings as last month. Three numbers changed and none of them concerns workers: the share of firms using AI was restated at 21.5% for July rather than 21.7% for the same month, a survey revision rather than adoption falling; a macro summary ticked down a tenth; one AI capability score fell on a re-scored pool.

The clearest support is that the weakness is specific to AI-exposed work. Information and professional services are shrinking outright, down 1.27% over the year, while construction, hospitality, education and health are growing 1.27%. In an ordinary downturn both sides fall together. But this gap alone cannot prove harm: growth of 1.27% in the untouched industries is exactly what absorbing everyone who left would look like. And the gap has stopped widening, sitting roughly flat across the last four readings.

The strongest thing against the displacement reading is job advertisements. Postings for knowledge work sit 23.1% below their February 2020 level while postings for hands-on work sit 5.3% above theirs — but the knowledge-work side has been recovering, closing about 2.6 points of that gap over six months. Postings lead actual hiring, this measure sorts by type of work rather than by industry, and it runs a month fresher than everything supporting the displacement reading. So the indicator that should turn first says demand for exposed work is stabilising.

Two further cautions. The entire twelve-month fall in prime-age employment happened in the last three months, on a household survey with known response problems in a year carrying a large one-off population revision — one noisy quarter is an ordinary thing to see. And layoffs, at 1.1% of employment, are low and still falling. Nobody is being pushed out; the burden is on people trying to get in.

Pay in exposed industries is growing faster than elsewhere, 4.63% against 3.53%, which looks like the opposite of displacement. I am not counting it. Following the same individuals over a year shows stayers at 3.6% and switchers at 4.4%, neither accelerating — the industry average is most likely rising because junior workers have left the denominator, not because anyone got a raise.

Watch the prime-age employment rate chart. A second quarter of decline confirms the last one was real; a flattening says it was noise.
