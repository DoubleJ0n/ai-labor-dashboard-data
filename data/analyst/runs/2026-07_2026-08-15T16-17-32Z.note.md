# Published note 2026-07 (run 2026-08-15T16:17:32Z)

Verdict: AUGMENTATION | 520 words (ordinary) | 17 numbers
Counts are reported, not enforced: length is proportional to what changed and the number ceiling is retired.
Notification: Prime-age employment flat at 80.4%; one panel updated, augmentation reading holds weakly.
Falsifier: The prime-age employment-rate chart moves first: if its 12-month change drops to −0.6 points or worse while control-industry job growth is still at or above 0.8 percent, the outflow from exposed work is no longer being absorbed and this reading is wrong.

---

The reading stays augmentation, and it rests on the absence of deterioration rather than on any evidence that AI is making workers more valuable. Nothing moved this fortnight. One panel produced a new number — pay growth for people who switch jobs, still 0.8 points above pay growth for people who stay — and it was unchanged from the month before. Every other series is where it was.

The strongest support is that employers are not pushing people out. Layoffs run at 1.1% of employment per month, lower than a year ago, and that comes from a survey of employers rather than households, so it is a genuinely separate instrument from the employment-rate figures. The share of Americans aged 25 to 54 with a job is 80.4%, unchanged over twelve months and about half a point above 2019. Hiring is soft — firms are hiring 3.4% of their workforce per month, low by past standards — but low hiring alongside low layoffs describes a frozen market, not a shedding one.

That matters because something real is happening in AI-exposed work. Employment in information, professional and business services and finance is 1.19% lower than a year ago — an outright contraction — while construction, hospitality and education and health added 1.16%. Employers are advertising about a quarter fewer knowledge-work roles than just before the pandemic, against hands-on postings running about 5% above theirs. Rates do not explain it: construction is the most interest-rate-sensitive industry there is, and it sits on the growing side.

Two things cut hard against this reading. First, a hiring freeze and a slow displacement running through the front door look identical from outside. If AI is eating entry-level knowledge work, the casualties are graduates who never get hired, who appear in no layoffs rate and vanish inside an employment rate covering everyone from 25 to 54. I cannot separate those from this data. Second, labour's share of national income has fallen to 51.0%, the lowest in the history of the series, down a full point in a year against a long-run drift of about a tenth of a point a year — roughly seven times the normal pace, and happening outside any recession, which is genuinely unusual. It is an economy-wide accounting ratio whose other side is corporate profits, so it is not evidence about AI-exposed work specifically. But it is also the oldest number here, from March, while the employment panels run through July: the strongest evidence against this reading is months staler than the evidence for it.

Nor is there positive evidence of gains. National productivity growth slowed to 2.2% from 2.9%. If AI were making people materially more productive, that is where it would show, and it decelerated instead. And the faster pay growth in AI-heavy industries — 4.22% against 3.21% — cannot be trusted as good news, because when entry-level hiring stops, the average wage rises simply because the juniors left.

What to watch: the prime-age employment rate. It is flat over twelve months but down three-tenths of a point over the last three, and that is the chart that would move first.
