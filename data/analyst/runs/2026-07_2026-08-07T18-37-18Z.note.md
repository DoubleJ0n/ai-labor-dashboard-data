# Published note 2026-07 (run 2026-08-07T18:37:18Z)

Notification: Reading flips to augmentation: prime-age employment share flat at 80.4%, layoffs low
Falsifier: The prime-age employment-population ratio chart moves first — if its 12-month change drops to -0.7 points or worse while control industries are still adding jobs at 0.8 percent or better, then the outflow from exposed work is not being reabsorbed and this reading was wrong.

---

The reading flips to augmentation this month, and it rests on the absence of deterioration rather than on evidence that AI is making workers more valuable. Nothing here shows augmentation working. What it shows is that the places where displacement would have to appear — people losing work and not finding new work — still do not show it, and that the one AI-specific measure that moved, moved slightly the right way. Confidence is low.

What changed: jobs in AI-exposed industries improved a little relative to the rest of the economy. Exposed industries are still shrinking, at 1.19% a year, while control industries add jobs at 1.16% — but the exposed side shrank less than it did last month. Productivity for the whole economy slowed to 2.2% growth from 2.9%. Everything else is unchanged, including the employment panel the reading actually turns on.

That panel is the share of 25-to-54-year-olds with a job: 80.4%, exactly where it was a year ago and about 0.45 points above its 2019 level. Layoffs are running at 1.1% of employment a month, low and lower than a year ago. Economy-wide, people who change employers are still landing at a pay premium over people who stay, 4.4% against 3.6%. If a category of work were being removed, this is where it would show, and it does not.

Against that, the number that cuts hardest: the share of national income going to workers rather than capital is 51.0%, the lowest in the record, down four quarters running and falling at roughly seven times its forty-year drift. Two things stop it from deciding the question, and neither is a dismissal. It covers every industry, so it cannot separate AI-exposed work from anything else — unlike the exposed-industry figures above, which cover a different and much smaller group of workers. And ordinary recessions have produced faster falls, 2.2 points in 2009. It is also the oldest figure here, from March, predating the July labour data. It stays on the watch list.

Three more things sit badly with the verdict. First, the standard benign explanation for exposed-industry weakness — that these industries over-hired in 2021-22 and have been unwinding since — no longer covers the present. We are four years past the hiring peak, an unwind should be closing, and instead exposed industries are contracting outright while job postings for knowledge work sit 23% below their February 2020 level with no recovery, against hands-on postings 5% above theirs. That residual is what keeps a displacement story alive.

Second, the market is frozen rather than healthy. Hiring is running at 3.4% of employment a month, low historically, and the improvement in the balance between hiring and quitting came from fewer people quitting, not more people being hired. Low layoffs plus low hiring means incumbents keep their jobs and the cost falls on people trying to get in — which is what an early, slow AI displacement would look like, and also what a high-rate, high-uncertainty economy looks like. This data cannot tell them apart.

Third, the flat employment share is doing less work than it appears. Prime-age employment actually fell over the year; the share held steady because the prime-age population fell too. That is a labour-supply story, most likely immigration, not a job-destruction story — but it is not the clean hold it looks like. And the gap between broad underemployment and headline unemployment, at 3.8 points and up 0.2 over the year, is the most deteriorated single reading on the board, though its last three months moved the other way.

One number I am deliberately not using: pay in exposed industries is growing 4.22% a year against 3.21% elsewhere, which looks like augmentation. But employment there is falling, and when entry-level hiring stops the remaining workforce skews senior and the average rises without anyone getting a raise. A real pay story and a bookkeeping artefact predict the same figure, so it counts for nothing in either direction.

What to watch: the prime-age employment chart. If its 12-month change turns down while control industries keep adding jobs, people leaving exposed work are not being reabsorbed, and this reading was wrong.
