The read stays displacement: AI-exposed industries keep losing jobs relative to the rest of the economy, but the data that should independently confirm displaced workers are failing to find new jobs isn't cooperating with that story this month, and that gap matters.

Almost nothing moved since the last check. The one number that changed at all was job postings for knowledge-work roles versus hands-on roles, which widened slightly from 28.8 points below their pre-pandemic level to 29.4 points below — essentially flat, not a fresh acceleration. Every other tracked figure sits exactly where it was.

The strongest case for the verdict: employment in AI-exposed industries (information, professional services, finance) fell 1.47% over the past year while everything else grew 1.4%, and that gap has kept widening for four straight readings rather than showing up as one bad month. This doesn't look like an ordinary rate-driven slowdown, because the comparison group includes construction, one of the most interest-rate-sensitive industries there is, and it's still growing.

The strongest case against it sits inside the very data meant to settle the question. Hiring is outrunning quitting by a growing margin, layoffs are down, and fewer people are stuck in part-time work against their wishes — three separate signs of a market that isn't shedding workers, all pointing the opposite direction from the headline. Only one supporting measure, the long-term unemployed share, still agrees with the displacement story, and even that improved over the most recent quarter. So this call rests more on the widening industry gap and a recent turn in the headline employment measure than on any confirmed failure of exposed workers to find new jobs — that confirmation still hasn't shown up.

One more thing worth naming plainly: pay in AI-exposed industries is growing faster than elsewhere, 4.0% against 3.19%, which sounds backwards for a displacement story. But exposed-industry employment is shrinking at the same time, and when lower-paid workers leave first, the average pay left behind rises on its own — this is a likely statistical artifact, not evidence of anything.

What to watch: the reabsorption panel's prime-age employment measure, and whether the hiring and layoffs numbers underneath it keep improving or start agreeing with the headline instead.
