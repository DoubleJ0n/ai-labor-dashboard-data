The reading is displacement, unchanged from last month. This is not a call resting on the absence of good news — it rests on a specific, dated change in how two different data series move together, described below. The confidence behind it is low: several independent measures show no comparable stress, and one of the panels central to the case cannot, by its own design, distinguish someone losing a job from someone leaving one voluntarily for something better.

What changed since last month: almost nothing. Two panels moved, and neither is large enough to shift the picture. Job postings in AI-exposed knowledge work (software, media, finance-adjacent white-collar roles) are still down 22.9% from where they stood in February 2020, against a 5% increase for hands-on control occupations over the same stretch — but the trend over the past six months has turned mildly less negative rather than more, a small point against displacement continuing to build. Separately, a benchmark tracking AI model capability (GDPval head-to-head ratings) dropped 89 points, but this was due to a change in the comparison pool the benchmark uses, not any change in what models can actually do, and it has no bearing on any worker's outcome — it is noted here only so it isn't mistaken for a capability regression.

The strongest evidence behind the displacement verdict is a change in shape, not a change in level. For over two years, AI-exposed industries have shown weaker job growth than comparable non-exposed industries while the broader labor market kept growing fine alongside it — a pattern consistent with workers reallocating out of shrinking niches into better jobs elsewhere, not being pushed out of the workforce. That pattern has broken. Exposed-industry employment is now down 1.47% over the past year while comparable non-exposed industries — construction, leisure and hospitality, education and health — grew 1.4% over the same period, a healthy pace that argues against this being an ordinary interest-rate story (construction is the most rate-sensitive industry in the economy and it is not struggling). At the same time, for five straight months now, the broader labor market has stopped absorbing workers as it should: the share of prime-age adults (25 to 54) with a job has fallen 0.3 percentage points over the past year, past the level this dashboard treats as an early sign of trouble. Exposed-sector weakness and broader labor-market softening moving together, rather than sitting side by side with an otherwise-healthy aggregate, is the reason this reads as displacement rather than reallocation this month.

Two qualifiers belong with that finding, at the same weight they carried in the underlying analysis. First, the prime-age employment rate, even after this decline, remains 0.45 percentage points above where it stood in 2019 — this is a softening off a strong base, not a collapse. Second, the exposed-versus-control jobs comparison cannot on its own tell the difference between mass layoffs and workers voluntarily leaving contracting industries for better opportunities; a labor market where everyone displaced from a shrinking niche instantly lands somewhere better would produce a very similar reading. That ambiguity is exactly why this call sits at low rather than high confidence.

The strongest evidence against the displacement verdict comes from three places that don't share the same weakness. A separate government survey of job openings and quits (JOLTS, independent of the household survey behind the employment-rate figures above) shows hiring minus quits unchanged over the past year and layoffs actually below their long-run norm — not what you'd expect if employers were actively shedding staff. A wage-growth measure that tracks the same individuals over time, rather than averaging across a changing mix of who's employed, shows workers who switch jobs still earning about 0.8 percentage points more than those who stay — slightly better than the historical norm, and not the depressed premium you'd expect if switchers were landing in worse positions. And the earliest-moving indicator on this dashboard, exposed-industry job postings, is flat to mildly improving over the past six months rather than deteriorating further. None of these numbers cover the same AI-exposed workers as the industry employment figures above — they are broader, economy-wide checks — so they don't overturn the case, but they mean it rests on one central finding rather than a consistent pattern across every measure.

What to watch: job postings for AI-exposed occupations move earliest of anything on this dashboard, before employment or wage data catch up, so that is the chart to check first for any real turn in either direction.
