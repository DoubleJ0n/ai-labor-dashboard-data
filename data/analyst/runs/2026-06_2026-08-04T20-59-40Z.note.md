---

The reading is displacement, held with low confidence, and it rests on deterioration that has actually shown up in the data rather than on an absence of good news.

Nothing moved this month. One of thirteen panels changed, and the change was a small downward revision to a job-postings figure already published for July, not a new observation. Everything below is last month's evidence read again.

What supports the displacement reading: employment in the industries most exposed to AI is shrinking, down 1.27% over the year, while industries AI has barely touched are adding jobs at 1.27%. Meanwhile the share of 25-to-54-year-olds with a job is 80.2%, down half a point over twelve months, and that fall comes from employment dropping by about 1.4 million rather than from a bigger population. A specific slice of the economy losing work while the wider market fails to absorb the outflow is what separates displacement from ordinary reshuffling. The mechanism is a hiring freeze, not firing: layoffs are low at 1.1 and falling, hiring is weak at 3.4, and 27.3% of the unemployed have now been out of work more than six months, up four points in a year. The cost is landing on people trying to get in and people already out.

What argues against it, and this is where the low confidence comes from: the freshest evidence on the dashboard disagrees. Job postings lead everything else here, and the shortfall in postings for exposed knowledge work — currently about 23% below its February 2020 level, against hands-on work sitting about 5% above its own — has been closing over the past six months, not widening. Much of that level is a hangover from the 2022 interest-rate shock. On a longer view the exposed-industry job shortfall was worse in late 2023, before AI tools diffused widely, and has narrowed while firm adoption climbed from 17.3% to 21.7% since November. A signal loudest before the tools arrived looks more like the 2021-22 over-hiring correction unwinding. Pay also runs the wrong way for displacement: exposed industries are paying 4.63% growth against 3.53% in control industries, though that could simply be entry-level hiring stopping and the remaining workforce skewing senior. Economy-wide, job switchers still earn 4.4% growth against 3.6% for stayers, which is opportunity-driven mobility, not distress — that figure covers the whole workforce, not exposed industries, so distress concentrated among a minority is exactly what it would miss. Productivity at 2.8% a year, against a pre-2020 norm near 1.3%, is the strongest sign pointing at augmentation, and it too is economy-wide.

One thing readers relying on the prime-age employment figure are owed: it may not be trustworthy. The entire twelve-month fall happened inside the most recent quarter, and the January population-control revision pushed a level shift of roughly 1.5 million into this survey — close to the 1.4 million employment drop doing the work here. The published ratio is meant to be consistent within a control regime and fell on both the quarterly and annual views, which is why I have not called this a confounder. But if that fall is revised away rather than reversed, the displacement reading loses its load-bearing fact. Separately, payrolls rose 57,000 in June and unemployment is 4.2%, both economy-wide, and neither looks like a market removing work at scale.

Watch the prime-age employment share chart. If it climbs back to flat or better against a year earlier, the market is catching exits again and this reading was wrong.
