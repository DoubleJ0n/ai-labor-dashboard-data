The reading is displacement, and it is a positive reading rather than a reading resting on the absence of something. Two things have to be true at once for me to say displacement rather than reshuffling, and both are on the board. First, something is happening specifically to AI-exposed work: employment in AI-exposed industries is shrinking by 1.27% over the past year while employment in the control industries AI has barely touched is growing by 1.27%. Second, the wider labour market is not catching the people coming out: the share of 25-to-54-year-olds with a job has fallen half a percentage point over twelve months, in a series that normally rises about four-tenths of a point a year, and the share of unemployed people who have been out of work six months or longer has climbed four points to 27.3%. If only the first were true, this would be reallocation — one set of industries shrinking, another growing, workers moving between them. It is the second that makes it displacement.

Nothing meaningful moved this month. The exposed-versus-control jobs gap shifted by four hundredths of a point, exposed and control pay growth each moved by a hundredth, the share of firms using AI was unchanged at 21.7%, and the postings gap closed by nine-tenths of a point. Every underlying labour reading is the same one as last month, because the household and payroll data still run through June. So the reading rests on levels that were already there, not on fresh deterioration, and I would rather say that plainly than dress a quiet month up as a development. The one genuinely new thing on the board is a measure of pay growth that follows the same individuals over time, and it argues against displacement. More on that below.

Taking the panels one at a time.

Jobs in exposed versus control industries. Naming both sides rather than the gap: the exposed side is in outright contraction, down 1.27% year over year, and the control side is growing at 1.27%. That combination is what rules out a general downturn. If this were the ordinary business cycle, construction, leisure and hospitality, and education and health would be weak too, and they are not — they are the ones adding jobs. Interest rates are not signalling recession either, though I am not leaning on that in either direction; a signal that says "no recession" cannot explain away weakness that is visibly there. What the control-industry strength buys me is specificity: whatever is hitting information and professional services is not hitting the whole economy. What it does not buy me is displacement over reallocation. That comes from the absorption evidence.

The strongest argument against the exposed-jobs finding is the hiring correction. Information and professional and business services hired far too aggressively in 2021 and 2022 and have been letting that unwind ever since. The postings data carries its own note saying the exposed-versus-control gap in job advertisements opened in 2022 and attributes a substantial part of it to the interest-rate cycle rather than to anything models did. That explains the opening of both gaps, and probably a fair part of why exposed employment is still below where its old trend would have put it in 2026. What it does not explain is why the gap is still here. An unwind decays. Four years on, the exposed side is not merely below trend, it is contracting year over year, while the controls — which also overhired, in leisure and hospitality — are growing. That leftover is what I am reading. It is a smaller thing than the raw two-and-a-half-point gap suggests, and I want that said rather than buried.

A limitation of the exposed-versus-control split worth stating: exposure is assigned by industry, not by job. A janitor in a software firm counts as exposed; a data analyst in a factory does not. The job-postings panel, which is cut by occupation, is the better measure of who is actually exposed — and it points the other way.

Job postings. This is the freshest panel on the board, running through July, a month ahead of everything supporting the displacement reading. Postings for knowledge work sit about 23% below their February 2020 level, while postings for hands-on work sit about 4% above theirs. That is a wide gap, and on level alone it supports the reading. But the level is old news and the movement is what matters: the gap has closed by 3.9 points over six months and by nine-tenths in the last month. Closing is what recovery, or genuine augmentation, would look like. There is a hole in this I cannot fill from what I have: I am given the change in the gap but not the change in each side separately, so I cannot tell whether knowledge-work postings are recovering or hands-on postings are falling back. If it is the latter, the narrowing is not good news at all. Either way, the panel most likely to turn first is turning against the displacement reading, and it is newer than the evidence for it. That is the single biggest reason I am not stating this with high confidence.

Absorption. The level of prime-age employment is not alarming — 80.2% of 25-to-54-year-olds have a job, which is fractionally above where it stood in 2019. The direction is what is bad. The twelve-month change is minus half a point, against a norm of gently rising. Almost the whole of that move happened in the most recent quarter, which means this is one quarter of evidence rather than a year of it, and one quarter can be noise.

I checked whether the employment rate is falling because employment is falling or because the population it is divided by is rising. Employment itself is down 1.38%, about 1.4 million prime-age people, so the ratio is not deteriorating merely on arithmetic. I am deliberately not quoting the population side: the January Census population controls put a plus-1.9-million level shift into this series in 2025 and a minus-1.5-million shift in 2026, which makes the population leg unusable for measuring anything. Only the direction of the employment leg survives, and that was the piece I needed.

The long-term unemployed share is the other absorption reading I lean on, at 27.3% and up four points on the year. It matters because it is a share of the unemployed rather than a share of the population, which makes it largely immune to the population-control problem that damages the headline employment rate. People who lose exposed-industry jobs are staying unemployed longer.

Against that, the flow numbers say something different and I have to take it. Layoffs are running at 1.1% of employment and are flat, exactly where they normally sit. Hiring is weak at 3.3% and quits at 1.9%, and the gap between them has actually improved slightly over the year. Weak hiring plus normal layoffs is not an economy shedding workers, it is an economy that has stopped taking people in. That is a frozen market, and a frozen market has a perfectly good non-AI explanation in interest rates and business uncertainty. It also puts most of the burden on people trying to enter work, who are mostly under 25 and therefore mostly outside the 25-to-54 group the headline employment rate covers. This is the strongest competing story and it is doing part of the work here. It fails the same specificity test the cyclical story fails — a rate-driven freeze should be general, and the control industries are hiring — which is why I record it as the main thing weakening confidence rather than as grounds to call this month confounded. Mixed evidence is not the same as an identified alternative cause.

Pay in exposed versus control industries. Pay in AI-exposed industries is growing faster than in control industries, 4.63% against 3.53%. On its face that is backwards for displacement. But this panel only means something when employment is holding up, and exposed employment is falling, so it cannot separate augmentation from displacement and I am not citing it as support for either. There is a specific reason to distrust it here: if entry-level hiring in exposed industries has stopped, the workforce that remains skews more senior and the average wage rises without anyone getting a raise. The economy-wide individual-level trackers sit at 3.6% for people who stay in their jobs and 4.4% for people who move — both well below 4.63% — which hints that the exposed-industry figure is partly composition. I cannot prove it, because those trackers cover the whole economy and cannot be cut to exposed industries. So the composition question stays open and the panel stays weak, in both directions.

The new panel: individual wage growth. This follows the same people over time, which is what makes it useful as a check on the composition problem above. People who change jobs are still earning about eight-tenths of a point more growth than people who stay, a premium above its pre-2020 average of about six-tenths, and it has been recovering over recent readings. That is the shape of people moving because they see something better, not because they were pushed. It is the cleanest single argument against the displacement reading, and unlike the productivity and labour-share numbers it cannot be dismissed as covering the wrong workers — it covers everyone, exposed workers included. Two things temper it without disposing of it. It is a median, so a collapse concentrated in a minority is precisely the shape it would miss. And the household survey behind it has been degrading, with October 2025 missing outright.

Productivity. Output per hour is growing at 2.8% a year, up from 2.5%, which sits at the low edge of the late-1990s internet-boom range for one quarter. Real aggregate gains showing up is what augmentation working looks like, and I take it seriously. Two things stop it from overturning the reading. It is economy-wide, so none of it can be attributed to AI-exposed work specifically. And output per hour is a ratio: it rises mechanically when hours are cut faster than output falls, so a firm producing the same with fewer people generates exactly this number. Rising productivity sitting on top of falling prime-age employment does not tell augmentation and displacement apart. This figure is also four months old, from the first quarter, against labour data running to June and July — a real gap, and worth flagging because this panel is the load-bearing piece of the augmentation counter-case.

Labour share of income. The share of national income going to workers rather than to capital has fallen to 51.0%, a new low for the full history, with four consecutive quarterly declines and a one-point fall over four quarters — a pace in the fastest tenth on record and many times the slow drift of the past forty years. Directionally this fits the displacement reading. I give it no weight in the verdict: it is economy-wide, it has no threshold, and on the historical record a measure like this fires in recessions, not on technology.

AI adoption. 21.7% of firms report using AI in some business function, up from 17.3% in November 2025, with large employers at 38.8% — so the share of workers in an AI-using firm is well above the share of firms. This establishes that AI use is now widespread enough that a labour effect is possible. It causes nothing and I am not treating its rise as evidence that anything has happened to workers.

Capability benchmarks. Read and counted in nothing. The leading model can now sustain tasks of a few hours' length at the measured reliability threshold, and on one professional-task benchmark models reached about 71% wins-plus-ties in December 2025 — though wins alone were just under half, and that is a lab reporting on its own benchmark with nothing published since. None of this is an event in anyone's employment.

Two alternatives I considered and rejected as the named cause. First, shrinking labour supply from immigration enforcement. If the prime-age population is genuinely falling, employment can fall with nobody being displaced. I reject it because the population series has been disqualified by its own documentation, as above, and because immigration-sensitive employment concentrates in construction and leisure and hospitality — the control industries, which are growing. A supply shock that spares the control side and hits information and professional services is the wrong shape. Second, the frozen hiring market, discussed above.

Two cross-panel oddities worth recording. The household survey says prime-age employment is down 1.38% over the year while the establishment survey says payrolls grew by 57,000 in the latest month and control industries grew 1.27% over the year. These are different surveys covering different things and it is wrong to net them against each other, but a disagreement that large is a reason to hold the household-based absorption reading loosely, particularly given the documented decline in household survey response rates. Separately, the headline employment rate's level says the market is fine and its change says it is deteriorating; that is expected, and the right way to read it is level for where we are, change for which way we are going. Overall the news picture is soft but positive: 57,000 jobs added, unemployment at 4.2%. A labour market destroying work wholesale does not usually add payrolls. That sits comfortably with the frozen-market story and uncomfortably with mine.

What would show the displacement reading is wrong. Absorption returning. Specifically: the prime-age employment rate climbing back so its twelve-month change is no worse than a tenth of a point down, while the long-term unemployed share falls back to 26% or below, by late October. The first says people are landing again; the second guards against a bounce that leaves the people already stranded still stranded. Both are reachable — the employment rate moved half a point in a single quarter, and the long-term share moved nearly two points in three months — and both publish monthly, so three more readings land before the deadline. I deliberately did not make the second test "the exposed-versus-control jobs gap closes." At its recent pace of movement it could not get to a meaningful level by October, and a test that cannot fire is worthless. It is also the wrong test: if people start landing while the industry gap stays open, that is reallocation with a functioning labour market, and reallocation is exactly the finding that would make a displacement reading wrong.
