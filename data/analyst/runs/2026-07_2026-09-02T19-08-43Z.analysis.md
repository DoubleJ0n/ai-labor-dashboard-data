The reading is AUGMENTATION again this month, and it rests on an absence of deterioration rather than on any positive sign that AI is making exposed workers more productive or better paid. That distinction matters for how to weigh everything below.

What changed since the last analysis: almost nothing. Two panels ticked. The gap between job postings in AI-exposed occupations and in hands-on control occupations widened slightly, by well under a percentage point, and a benchmark tracking how often AI systems match or beat human experts on real work tasks edged up further. Neither move is large enough to shift the call. Every other panel, including the two that actually decide this verdict, is unchanged from last month.

The strongest thing supporting the augmentation reading is the employment path for prime-age workers, 25 to 54 years old. This is the panel built to answer the destruction-versus-reallocation question directly, and over the past 12 months the share of that group employed hasn't moved at all, and now sits above where it stood before the pandemic. That is not what a labor market shedding workers into unemployment looks like. Two independent JOLTS readings back this up: employers are not laying people off at an elevated pace, and hiring relative to quitting looks ordinary. Whatever is happening in AI-exposed work is not showing up yet as people losing jobs and failing to find new ones.

The strongest thing against it is that the picture directly underneath that flat 12-month figure is not flat. Looked at over just the last three months, the same employment ratio is falling, and falling at a pace that's starting to look unusual by its own historical standards. That faster, more recent read disagrees with the 12-month figure that currently decides the call. If that three-month pace continues through the next print or two, the 12-month number it feeds into will follow it down, and the reallocation story flips to a displacement one. Alongside that, the industries most exposed to AI just crossed a line worth noting for the first time: employment in those industries is shrinking year-over-year while employment in comparable non-exposed industries is growing at an ordinary clip. That gap has been sitting at roughly this level for four months without moving further, which is why it doesn't yet override the flat employment ratio, but it is the newest piece of the puzzle and it is not a small one. There is also a softer signal worth naming honestly: the share of workers stuck in part-time work who'd rather have full-time hours is close to a level this dashboard treats as a warning sign, and it has risen over the past year even though its own most recent three months are calmer. If displacement is happening but showing up as underemployment rather than unemployment, this is where it would appear first, and a flat headline ratio wouldn't capture it.

Two other threads deserve mention because they look like evidence but aren't clean. Pay in AI-exposed industries is growing faster than in the rest of the economy, 4.2% versus 3.2% over the past year, which on its face looks like AI raising the value of those workers. But employment in those same industries is shrinking, and shrinking industries mechanically pay their survivors more even with no change in anyone's underlying value, since the people most easily let go tend to be lower earners. This dashboard can't separate those two stories from the wage numbers alone. A separate, economy-wide measure of individual pay growth (not cut to exposed versus non-exposed industries, so not directly comparable to the industry figures above) shows nothing unusual, which argues against a dramatic distortion but doesn't resolve the exposed-industry question either way.

I also checked and rejected a specific alternative explanation for the exposed-industry jobs weakness: an early-cycle slump led by temporary staffing services, which shows up in the same industry data as "professional and business services" and has nothing to do with AI. If that were driving things, layoffs should be elevated and hiring soft. Neither is true right now, so this explanation doesn't fit the current data, though it remains worth checking again each month.

Economy-wide productivity growth slowed to 2.2% year-over-year this quarter from 2.9% the quarter before (this is an economy-wide figure and not specific to AI-exposed industries, unlike the jobs and wage numbers above). That's a small deceleration, not the kind of acceleration you'd expect to see if augmentation were visibly paying off in aggregate output. It doesn't argue for displacement, but it removes any claim that augmentation is showing up as measurable gains yet. Adoption of AI tools among firms keeps climbing, concentrated more heavily among larger employers, which establishes that the tools are in wider use but says nothing by itself about what that use is doing to workers. Capability benchmarks continue to improve and the split between automating tasks outright versus augmenting a worker doing them has held steady, but these are all measures of what the technology can do, not measures of what has happened to anyone's job, and some of the readings behind them are close to a year old.

Confidence in this call is low, for three reasons that all point the same direction: the exposed-industry jobs gap crossing its attention line is brand new and sits on the least reliable part of the underlying government data, which tends to get revised in the following months; the three-month pace of the prime-age employment ratio disagrees with the 12-month figure the call is built on; and the underemployment measure is close to its own warning level. None of these breaks the call on its own, but together they make this a thin reading worth checking again next month rather than a settled one.
