---

The augmentation verdict holds this month: there is still no sign that AI is displacing workers faster than the economy can absorb them. That reading rests mainly on an absence of deterioration, not on positive proof that AI is making exposed workers more productive — worth saying plainly, because it changes how much weight the verdict can bear.

What's new is almost nothing. Since last month, a broad financial-conditions measure eased slightly (0.5 to 0.4) and an AI-capability benchmark ticked up two points (1822 to 1824). Neither matters. Every other panel behind this call — jobs, wages, absorption, productivity — carries the same figures it carried last month.

The strongest support: the share of prime-age adults (25-54) who are employed was flat over the past year and sits above its pre-pandemic level, while layoffs are running below their historical norm — people already in jobs are not being pushed out. The weakness that exists is concentrated specifically in AI-exposed industries: hiring in industries AI barely touches is running well above its own historical pace, so a general economic slowdown doesn't explain what's happening in exposed industries.

The strongest thing against it is two-sided. First, the flat employment figure above is a 12-month number, which moves slowly; the same measure's faster 3-month reading is already pointing down and disagrees with the flat headline — something that would move first is already leaning the other way, it just hasn't dragged the slower number down yet. Second, and more direct: economy-wide productivity growth — the number that would show AI-driven gains actually showing up — has been decelerating, not accelerating, slipping from 2.9% to 2.2% over the past year, the slowest pace of this stretch. If AI were successfully augmenting exposed workers at scale, this number should be rising. Separately, pay in AI-exposed industries is growing faster than in unexposed industries, but that gap can't be trusted this month: exposed employment is shrinking, so it may just reflect lower-paid workers leaving those industries rather than a real wage gain, and a more reliable economy-wide measure of pay growth for job-switchers is flat, showing no bidding war for movers.

What to watch: the employment-absorption panel's faster 3-month trend against its slower 12-month headline — if that recent softness persists, the flat number now supporting this call will turn negative too.
