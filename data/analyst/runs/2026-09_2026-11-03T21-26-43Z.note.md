This month's reading is DISPLACEMENT: AI-exposed industries are shrinking while other industries keep growing, and the economy-wide data on whether those workers are landing elsewhere does not yet show a clear answer either way.

What's new is almost nothing in the numbers themselves. Nearly every panel printed the same figures as last month — exposed-industry jobs still down 1.6% over the year against a 1.19% gain in control industries, exposed-industry postings still running well below their pre-pandemic level, the share of prime-age adults employed still flat over the year. The only series that moved was a macro-health check, and it moved by a hair, staying in normal territory. The verdict flipped from AUGMENTATION last month to DISPLACEMENT this month on essentially unchanged data — a sign of how close this call is, not a sign that anything broke this month.

The strongest fact behind DISPLACEMENT: employment in AI-exposed industries is contracting (down 1.6% over the year) while employment in non-exposed control industries is growing (up 1.19%), and a separate dataset built from job postings tells the same story — postings for knowledge work sit about 22 points below their pre-pandemic level while postings for hands-on work sit about 8 points above theirs. Two independent measurements agree the exposed side is weak while the unexposed side is healthy, which argues against a general downturn and for something specific to AI-exposed work. One honest caveat on that postings gap: it opened in 2022 during the rate-hiking slowdown and has barely moved in six months, so it's better described as an old wound that stopped healing than as fresh, ongoing damage.

The strongest fact against DISPLACEMENT, and the reason this call is held with low confidence: the measure built specifically to show whether laid-off workers are failing to find other jobs — the share of prime-age adults employed — has not fallen at all over the past year, and its most recent three-month trend is actually improving. If AI were pushing exposed-industry workers out of the labor force faster than the economy could absorb them, this is the chart that should move first, and right now it is moving the opposite way.

One more figure needs a flag rather than a face-value read: pay in exposed industries is rising faster than in control industries, but because exposed-industry employment is shrinking, that could simply reflect lower-paid workers leaving the group rather than real raises — the data cannot currently tell the two apart.

What to watch: the share of prime-age adults employed, the measure of whether exposed-industry workers are being reabsorbed elsewhere.
