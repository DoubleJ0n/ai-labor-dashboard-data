This month's reading stays where it was last month: the data point toward augmentation, meaning AI appears to be changing how existing jobs work rather than eliminating them wholesale. That reading rests mainly on the absence of a specific kind of bad news rather than on direct proof that AI is making workers more productive or valuable. It is worth saying plainly, because it changes what the verdict can and can't claim.

What moved since last month

Almost nothing. Of the eleven indicators tracked, nine are completely unchanged from the prior release. Two shifted, both by a small amount and both in the less favorable direction. The gap between job postings in AI-exposed industries and job postings in comparable but less-exposed industries widened slightly further, from a 30-point spread to a 30.4-point spread. And the yield curve, a general recession indicator, eased slightly from +0.5 to +0.4 points - still far from an inverted curve that would signal recession risk, but a touch softer. Neither change is large enough on its own to matter, but together with an unchanged core picture, this is a flat month with one quiet continuation of a worry already on the table.

The case for augmentation

The dashboard's method for separating "workers displaced and not finding new jobs" from "workers moving between jobs" relies on one number above all: the prime-age (25 to 54) employment rate, because this age group isn't affected by retirement trends that complicate the overall data. That rate has not fallen over the past twelve months, and it currently sits about three-quarters of a point above its pre-pandemic (2019) level - a solid floor, not a plateau built on weakness. Separately, two labor-market indicators that use a different government data source (job openings and hires, not household surveys) tell a consistent story: hiring is outpacing quits, and layoffs are trending down over the past year. None of these are booming numbers, but none show a labor market that's freezing up or shedding workers who can't find new jobs.

At the same time, employment in AI-exposed industries (jobs in fields like customer service, writing-heavy professional work, and similar categories) is down about 1.6% from a year ago, while employment in comparable, less-exposed control industries is up about 1.2%. That gap has held past the dashboard's own warning line for two consecutive months, which is itself a real finding - something specific is happening to exposed industries that isn't happening economy-wide, since the control industries are growing, not just failing to shrink as much. The method here is to combine these two readings using a rule built into the dashboard itself: when the exposed-industry gap is widening or holding wide, but the reabsorption measure (the prime-age employment rate) isn't cracking, the likely explanation is that workers are moving across industries rather than being pushed out of work altogether. That's what's driving the augmentation call. It is explicitly a call that something is shrinking in AI-exposed work, but that the people involved are, so far, landing somewhere else.

I considered and set aside two other explanations. A general economic slowdown doesn't fit, because the control industries are growing at a healthy pace, not just holding steady - a broad slump would normally pull them down too. A specific one-off event - a strike, a single-industry shock - doesn't fit either, because the industries affected here (information, professional services, financial services) include several different sub-sectors, and there's no current, dated 2026 event that explains a drop across all of them simultaneously. Past events like the 2023 entertainment-industry strikes are too old to explain September 2026 numbers.

The case against, and the open wound

The strongest evidence cutting against this verdict is job postings. Postings for jobs in AI-exposed industries sit about 22% below their pre-pandemic (February 2020) level, while postings in control industries are about 9% above theirs - a wide and, this month, still slightly widening gap. Job postings typically move before actual hiring and employment do, which makes this the panel most likely to show the first sign of a turn, in either direction. If augmentation were actually paying off - companies using AI to get more out of existing exposed-industry workers and hence hiring more of them, not fewer - this is the chart where that recovery should appear first. It hasn't. The six-month trend in this gap is roughly flat with a slight further softening, not a turn for the better. This is an unresolved doubt against the verdict, not something the rest of the data explains away.

A second loose thread: among the reabsorption panel's secondary readouts, the share of unemployed workers who have been out of work long-term rose 3.5 points over the past year to 27.1%, moving in the wrong direction even though it hasn't yet crossed the dashboard's own attention line for that measure. It is the one secondary reading inside an otherwise calm reabsorption picture that isn't calm.

Other panels, briefly

Wages in AI-exposed industries are growing faster than in control industries (4.1% versus 3.4% over the past year), which would normally look like augmentation paying off. But because employment in those same exposed industries is shrinking, this reading is genuinely ambiguous: if less-productive or junior workers are the ones losing jobs, the remaining workforce's average pay rises for reasons that have nothing to do with AI making anyone more valuable. A separate, economy-wide measure that isn't affected by this problem - wage growth for people who stay in their jobs versus people who switch jobs - shows both groups doing reasonably well (3.5% and 4.4%), which doesn't confirm the exposed-industry story but doesn't contradict it either. I'm treating the exposed-industry wage gap as unresolved, not as support.

Economy-wide productivity growth, the one place a genuine AI augmentation payoff would eventually have to show up in the aggregate, decelerated to 2.2% from 2.9% in the most recent reading (data is from June, several months old). It's still above long-run historical averages, but a deceleration is the opposite of what a strong augmentation story would predict, which is part of why this verdict carries low rather than moderate confidence.

On the mechanism side, AI adoption among firms keeps climbing - 23.8% of firms reporting use, up from 17.3% less than a year earlier, with larger employers adopting faster. This confirms AI deployment is real and growing, which rules out "there's no mechanism for anything to be happening." It says nothing about whether that deployment is costing or helping any specific worker, since the survey measures firm-level adoption, not employment outcomes.

Why confidence stays low

This verdict is augmentation, not confounded and not displacement, but held with low confidence for four reasons: the support is largely the absence of a downturn in reabsorption rather than positive proof of augmentation; the job postings panel, which should move first if the picture is turning, shows no recovery; the long-term unemployment share is drifting the wrong way even if it hasn't crossed its line; and the one place aggregate augmentation gains should show up - productivity growth - is slowing rather than accelerating.

What would change the call

The published note names the single chart to watch. The fuller condition: this verdict would flip if the prime-age employment rate starts falling by at least a tenth of a point over twelve months while the exposed-versus-control jobs gap remains past its current warning line - together, that combination would mean exposed-industry job losses are no longer being absorbed elsewhere.
