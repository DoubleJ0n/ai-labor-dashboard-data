The bottom line has not moved: this remains an augmentation call, meaning the data still show no sign that AI is displacing workers faster than the economy can absorb them. It is important to be precise about what kind of evidence that rests on. This is not a case where productivity or wages show AI making exposed workers visibly more valuable. It is a case where the labor-market numbers that would show harm simply are not showing it yet. That is an absence-of-deterioration finding, not a positive augmentation-working finding, and the two should not be confused.

What changed since last month: almost nothing. Of the panels behind this call, only two data points ticked at all. A broad measure of financial-market conditions eased slightly (from 0.5 to 0.4), and an AI-capability benchmark rose two points (1822 to 1824). Neither is large enough to move the read in either direction. Every other panel behind this call — the exposed-versus-control jobs gap, the prime-age employment measure, the wage comparisons, job postings, productivity — carries the identical figures it carried last month.

The case for augmentation, in detail: the share of prime-age adults (25 to 54) who are employed was flat over the past 12 months, and it sits above where it stood before the pandemic. The rate of layoffs is running below its own historical norm, which means the softness visible elsewhere is not showing up as employers actively shedding workers. And the weakness that does exist is concentrated specifically in AI-exposed industries, not spread economy-wide: industries AI has barely touched are hiring well above their own historical pace, while AI-exposed industries are running well below theirs. That split argues against a general economic slowdown being the explanation — a broad slowdown would hit both groups, and it isn't.

The case against, in full: three things. First, the reabsorption measure that anchors the "absorption is holding" claim is a 12-month figure, which is slow by construction. Its own faster, 3-month reading is already pointing down and disagrees with the flat 12-month number. If that recent softness continues for another month or two, the flat headline will itself turn negative — this is the specific place a turn would show up first, and it has already started to lean that way underneath the surface number. Second, a separate underemployment measure — people working part-time who want full-time work, relative to the official unemployed — has been drifting up over the past year and sits close to a level that would raise real concern, even though its most recent three months look better. Third, and most direct: economy-wide productivity growth, the one number that would show AI-driven output gains actually materializing, has been decelerating rather than accelerating — down from 2.9% to 2.2% year over year, the slowest pace in this stretch. If AI were successfully augmenting exposed workers' output at scale, this is the number that should be climbing, and it is doing the opposite.

A fourth item deserves a specific caveat rather than being counted either way. Pay in AI-exposed industries has been growing a bit faster than in unexposed industries (a gap that widened from roughly 0.9 to 1.0 percentage points). Taken alone, that looks like evidence against displacement. But exposed-industry employment is shrinking at the same time, and when a workforce is shrinking, faster-looking pay growth can simply mean lower-paid workers are leaving the roster, not that anyone's pay is actually rising faster. A separate, more reliable measure — pay growth for people who switch jobs, tracked as the same individuals a year apart across the whole economy — is flat and running below its own historical average, showing no bidding war for movers. Because these two measures disagree, the wage evidence is being treated as uninformative this month, not as support for augmentation.

Two plausible alternative explanations for the exposed-industry weakness were considered and set aside for lack of evidence, not disproven. Temporary-staffing employment, which sits inside the "exposed industries" grouping and tends to soften early in any slowdown, could be inflating the exposed-industry weakness; and the entertainment industry's 2023 labor disputes still echo in the same industry grouping. Both are real limitations on how cleanly "exposed industries" maps onto "AI-exposed work," but there is no separate data series available this month to size either effect, so neither is being invoked as the explanation — it is a caveat on how the exposed-versus-control split should be read, not a finding.

Confidence in this call is low. The reassuring reading on absorption leans heavily on figures drawn from one survey (the household survey), with only one measure from a separate source — the layoffs rate — corroborating it. And the fact that the reabsorption measure's own faster-moving component is already softer than its slower headline is an unresolved doubt sitting inside the strongest supporting panel, not outside it.
