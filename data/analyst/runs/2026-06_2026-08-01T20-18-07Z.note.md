---

The reading is displacement, and it is a weak one. It rests on deterioration that is actually present, not on the absence of improvement: prime-age employment (ages 25 to 54) is down about 1.4 million over twelve months in the household survey, and the share of the unemployed who have been out of work more than six months has risen 4 points in a year, to 27.3 percent.

Nothing changed this month. Not one panel's headline number moved. This is the same picture read again, not a new development.

The strongest support is the split between industries. Employment in the industries AI touches most — information, professional services, finance — is down 1.27 percent over the year, while employment in hands-on industries like construction, health care and hospitality is up 1.27 percent. One side is shrinking outright; the other is growing healthily. A general slowdown does not spare construction and hospitality while hollowing out office work. Note, though, that this gap is not widening: it was slightly wider a quarter ago, and at its 2023 worst it was more than twice as large. The news is persistence, not acceleration. Some of it is still the unwinding of the 2021-22 over-hiring in those same industries — but four years on, that unwind leaves exposed employment falling in absolute terms rather than merely growing slower, and that residual is what this reading is built on.

The strongest thing against it is fresher than the evidence for it. Job postings move before headcount, and the postings data runs a month ahead of the employment data here. Postings for knowledge work sit 22.8 percent below their February 2020 level while postings for hands-on work sit 5.3 percent above theirs — but that gap has narrowed over the past six months. Much of the level is a 2022 interest-rate event still sitting in place. A narrowing gap is not what an accelerating displacement episode produces. It is not augmentation either: that would mean knowledge-work postings climbing back toward pre-COVID levels, and being nearly a quarter below is not that.

Two further doubts. Productivity across the whole economy is growing 2.8 percent a year, up from 2.5 percent, matching the late-1990s boom — but it covers all workers, not just AI-exposed ones, it is five months old, and output per hour also rises simply when low-productivity hours are cut. And people who switch jobs are still getting bigger raises than people who stay, 4.4 percent against 3.6, which is what opportunity-driven moving looks like rather than distress.

One contradiction a reader should know about. The employer survey says the economy added 57,000 jobs in June with unemployment at 4.2 percent, while the household survey says prime-age employment is shrinking. They measure different things and neither refutes the other, but the household survey is the one with known response problems, and almost the entire twelve-month fall happened in a single quarter. If the employer survey is the truer picture, this displacement reading is wrong.

Finally, on character: layoffs are normal at 1.1 percent and flat, while hiring is weak. That is a frozen market, not one shedding people. Incumbents are not being pushed out; the burden falls on entrants and on anyone who leaves. A frozen market is also exactly what "displacement hasn't started, employers are just cautious" looks like from outside, and this data cannot fully rule that out. The level of prime-age employment also remains about a quarter of a point above 2019 — the flow has turned, the stock has not.

What to watch: the prime-age employment-rate chart. If its twelve-month change climbs back toward flat, people leaving exposed work are being reabsorbed and this displacement reading fails.
