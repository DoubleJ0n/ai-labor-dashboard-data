Falsifier: The prime-age employment-rate chart moves first — if its twelve-month change drops to -0.8 points or worse while control-industry job growth is still at or above 0.5 percent, absorption has failed in a way that is specific to AI-exposed work and this reading was wrong.

---

The reading is still augmentation, and it rests on the absence of deterioration rather than on any evidence that AI is making workers more valuable: the aggregate labour market is holding up, not visibly improving for the people most exposed.

Nothing moved this month. One figure changed — the survey of firms using AI revised its July reading from 21.5% to 21.8% of firms, a correction to a month already published, not a new observation. Every other panel carries the same number as last time.

What supports the reading: in the industries AI has barely touched — construction, hospitality and similar hands-on work — employment is up 1.16% on the year, and postings for hands-on work sit about 5% above their February 2020 level. Meanwhile the share of 25-54-year-olds with a job is 80.4%, unchanged over twelve months and still about half a point above 2019. Layoffs are low and falling. Firms are hiring faster than workers are quitting. People who switch jobs are still paid a premium for switching, 4.4% pay growth against 3.6% for stayers — though that is an economy-wide figure covering all workers, not the AI-exposed ones. So the weakness in exposed work is specific, not a general slump: a downturn would drag the healthy side down too, and it has not.

What argues against it, and it is serious. Employment in AI-exposed industries — information, professional and business services, finance — is not just growing slowly, it is shrinking, down 1.19% on the year. At the same time layoffs across the economy are falling. Put those together and those jobs are vanishing through people leaving and not being replaced. The harm from that lands on people who never got hired, and the prime-age employment rate — which measures whether people who already have jobs keep them — is close to blind to them. Two measures pick up traces: the share of the unemployed out of work more than six months is 25.5%, up 0.6 points over the year, and the gap between broad underemployment and headline unemployment has widened to 3.8 points, the signature of people landing in worse work while the job count holds. Postings for knowledge work remain about 24% below their February 2020 level, six years on, while hands-on postings are above theirs.

Labour's share of national income is 51.0%, down a full point over four quarters against a long-run drift of about a tenth of a point a year, and the lowest in a record starting in 1947. That is what capital substituting for labour looks like. It covers the whole economy, so it cannot be pinned on AI-exposed work, it has been drifting down for forty years, and recessions produced faster falls — but it is a record low falling at near-recession speed without a recession, and it is the oldest number here, five months behind the job data.

Two cautions on the numbers themselves. The recent improvement in the exposed-versus-comparison jobs gap sits entirely inside the two most recent payroll months, which are preliminary and revise most, and revise downward when a downturn is starting. And the employment panel's underlying counts of employed people show a fall that sits oddly beside a flat employment rate: that is the annual January re-basing of population figures, not economic news, so the rate is the reliable number and the raw counts this year are not.

What to watch: the prime-age employment rate chart — the share of 25-54-year-olds with a job. It is flat over twelve months but down 0.3 points over three, and if the shorter window is the truth, this reading is wrong.
