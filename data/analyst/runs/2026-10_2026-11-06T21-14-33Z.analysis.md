The verdict stays AUGMENTATION this month. That word needs a caveat attached every time it's used: this reading rests on the absence of harm to AI-exposed workers, not on evidence that AI is making anyone more productive or valuable. Nothing in this month's data shows exposed workers earning a premium for their skills, getting hired back faster, or any output measure accelerating because of AI. What the data shows is that the labor market has not yet failed to absorb whatever shift is happening in AI-exposed industries — a lower bar, and worth being honest about.

What moved since last month: two numbers, in opposite directions, plus one that didn't move at all and matters more for that reason.

First, the job-growth gap between AI-exposed industries and industries AI barely touches (construction, leisure, education and health) narrowed from 2.79 percentage points to 2.52 points. Exposed industries are still growing jobs more slowly than the rest of the economy, but the gap eased rather than widened, which is the third straight month it's sat at a level the dashboard treats as worth watching closely, even as it eased this time rather than deepening.

Second, the pay gap between the same two groups nearly vanished. Exposed-industry pay growth had been running 0.66 points ahead of control industries; now the two are within 0.08 points of each other, essentially tied. On its own this could read as reassuring — AI-exposed workers aren't losing ground on pay. But employment in those same exposed industries is falling, about 1.35% over the past year, which means the pool of workers left in the pay statistics is shrinking and skewing toward whoever stayed. A falling headcount and a genuine pay shift produce the same observed number, and this dataset cannot tell them apart. So the collapse in the pay gap is reported as what it is — a large one-month move — without treating it as proof either way.

Third, and unchanged: economy-wide productivity growth came in at 2.1%, down slightly from 2.3%, the second straight quarter below the pace associated with earlier technology-driven productivity booms (2.7% to 3.4%). If augmentation were visibly "working" in the strong sense — AI making the existing workforce more productive at scale — this is the number that would be rising. It is not; it is drifting down. This is the clearest reason not to describe the current reading as augmentation succeeding. It is better described as displacement not yet starting.

The strongest evidence behind the verdict comes from the reabsorption data — the panel built specifically to check whether workers pushed out of AI-exposed roles are failing to find other work. The share of prime-age adults (25 to 54) who are employed was flat over the past twelve months and sits three-quarters of a point above its pre-pandemic level. Layoffs are running below their historical norm, not above it — this is not a market where incumbent workers are being cut loose. Hiring has cooled somewhat relative to quitting, but hiring still outpaces quitting, and the pool of people stuck in long spells of unemployment has grown only modestly over the year. Put together, this looks like a frozen hiring market — tough on people trying to get in, not a market actively ejecting people who are already in. That distinction matters, and it is the reason the attribution-side alarm (the exposed-versus-control jobs gap) is not being read as proof of displacement on its own: a frozen, cautious labor market and a displacement event can produce a similar-looking jobs gap, and only the reabsorption data can tell them apart. Right now it says frozen, not shedding.

The strongest evidence against the verdict — and the one piece that should carry full weight rather than being softened — is job postings. Postings for AI-exposed occupations sit 21.6% below their pre-pandemic level; postings for less-exposed occupations sit 8.1% above theirs. That roughly 30-point gap opened in 2022, largely as a rate-driven hiring slowdown rather than an AI story at the time, and it has been completely flat for six months: no further deterioration, but also no healing. This is the most direct, fastest-moving proxy for AI-driven hiring weakness on the dashboard, and it is not telling a "things are fine" story. It is telling a "nothing has changed" story — a standing injury to knowledge-work hiring that has neither gotten worse nor gone away. If a new wave of AI-driven disruption were starting, postings is where it would likely show up first, and it has not shown up there, but the existing damage from whatever caused the original 2022 split has also not repaired itself after four years. Either reading is uncomfortable for a clean "augmentation is working" story.

One more detail is worth surfacing rather than glossing over. Inside the reabsorption data, the share of unemployed people who have been out of work long-term rose more over the past three months than its own twelve-month trend would predict — a short-term wobble that moved past the level the dashboard treats as worth attention, even though the twelve-month reabsorption number itself has not moved. This is the single most likely place for the current reading to start turning, and it has not yet shown up in the headline number that the verdict depends on.

Other things checked and set aside: economy-wide wage growth for people who switch jobs is healthy (4.4% for switchers against 3.5% for people who stay in place), which argues the broader pay backdrop isn't collapsing, but this is an economy-wide number and cannot confirm or rule out a worse experience specifically among exposed-industry switchers. Labor's overall share of national income is falling at one of the faster paces on record, but this trend predates AI by decades and is treated as too ambiguous to count either way. AI adoption among firms continues to climb, now reported near 24% and concentrated in larger employers, which keeps the mechanical case for an eventual displacement story open but says nothing about whether it has started. None of these moved the verdict.

Taken together: the reading stays AUGMENTATION because the panel built to detect a reabsorption failure has not found one, not because any panel shows AI-exposed workers being made more valuable. The postings gap staying stuck, and the early wobble in long-term unemployment, are the two places where a different story could start to show up, and neither should be read as resolved just because the headline reabsorption number hasn't moved yet.
