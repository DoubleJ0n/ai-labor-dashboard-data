The augmentation reading holds this month, but the more honest label for it is "displacement hasn't started yet," not "AI is making workers more valuable." That distinction matters because the case rests entirely on the absence of bad news in one measure, not on any positive evidence that exposed workers are better off.

Almost nothing changed in the underlying data this month. The one update was the share of firms using AI in any business function, which rose to 25% from 24.2%. Every other reading — industry employment, job postings, wages, the labor-market reabsorption measure — came in exactly where it stood last time.

The strongest support for the verdict: the share of prime-age adults (25 to 54) who are employed hasn't moved in 12 months and sits above its 2019 level, while layoffs are low and still falling. People leaving jobs, including in AI-exposed industries, are not visibly failing to find new ones.

The strongest thing against it: AI-exposed industries lost 1.35% of their jobs over the past year while a matched set of less-exposed industries gained 1.17%, and that gap has held at roughly the same elevated level for three straight months rather than closing. The freshest data available, job postings by occupation as of November, shows postings for AI-heavy knowledge work 21% below pre-pandemic levels against postings for hands-on work running 10% above theirs, and that gap kept widening over the last six months — well past the point where a 2022 rate shock could still explain it. A separate anomaly worth naming without softening: the pay premium that AI-exposed industries used to command, about 0.7 points faster growth than elsewhere, has nearly vanished, down to 0.08 points, even though workers leaving an industry from the bottom should, mechanically, push that number up rather than down. That compression doesn't have a clean explanation in this data.

One caveat on the supportive side: some of the apparent strength in less-exposed industries may be concentrated in health care, which moves on public budgets rather than the business cycle, narrowing the comparison somewhat. It doesn't reverse the picture, since the other less-exposed industries checked show no comparable weakness.

What to watch: the job postings gap between AI-exposed and hands-on occupations, since it is the freshest data available and the only piece of this picture still moving in the adverse direction right now.
