---

The reading is still displacement, and still weak. It rests on one thing deteriorating, not on the absence of harm: the share of 25-to-54-year-olds with a job has fallen half a point over the past year, to 80.2%, and the number of prime-age people actually employed is down about 1.4 million, so this is not a ratio distorted by a growing population. That is an economy-wide figure. This is not augmentation working — nothing here shows AI-exposed work expanding.

Almost nothing moved since last month. The jobs and pay comparisons, the employment decline, productivity, the labour share and the adoption figures are all unchanged. The job-ads number changed from -27.1 to -28.1, but the month it refers to did not change, so that was a revision to an already-published reading, not the gap widening. Treat this as a re-reading of a standing situation, not news.

The strongest support is that the damage is specific rather than general. Employment in AI-exposed industries is shrinking outright, down 1.3% over the year, while industries AI has barely touched added jobs at 1.3%. Job ads for knowledge-work occupations sit about 23% below their February 2020 level; ads for hands-on occupations sit about 5% above theirs. Those cover different groups of workers, which is the point: a broad slump would drag the untouched side down too, and it has not. Alongside that, 27.3% of the unemployed have now been out of work more than six months, up 4 points over the year. Layoffs are at their normal rate and unmoved, while hiring is weak. Nobody is being pushed out; firms have simply stopped replacing people, and the cost lands on those trying to get in.

The strongest argument against is that the AI-specific gaps are healing, not widening. The jobs gap between exposed and untouched industries narrowed slightly and has been flat across four readings, and it is less than half as wide as at its worst in late 2023. The job-ads gap has closed by about 2.8 points over six months, and job ads usually move first. The obvious non-AI explanation covers most of it: information and professional services over-hired in 2021 and 2022 and have been unwinding ever since, which predates today's tools. What that explanation does not cover is thin but real — we are three and a half years past that hiring peak and exposed employment is still contracting outright, not merely growing slowly, while firm adoption of AI has climbed from 17.3% in November 2025 to 21.7% now, and to 38.8% among firms with over 250 employees. A correction still running this long needs its own explanation. That residual is why this reading survives, and its thinness is why confidence is low.

Three further cautions. Job switchers are still getting paid more to move, 4.4% against 3.6% for stayers — not what a displacement episode eventually produces, though a healthy middle can hide a minority landing badly. June payrolls rose 57,000 with unemployment at 4.2%: soft, but not contraction. And the whole of the prime-age employment drop happened in the last three months, in a household survey with known response problems, from a level still slightly above 2019. Where things stand is fine; the direction is not.

Productivity growth of 2.8% is often read as the augmentation counterweight. It is not usable as one: output per hour also rises when hours fall faster than output, so labour shedding produces the same print, and this figure is a quarter older than the employment data.

What to watch: the prime-age employment-rate chart. If its twelve-month change climbs back toward flat, people are landing again and this displacement reading was wrong.
