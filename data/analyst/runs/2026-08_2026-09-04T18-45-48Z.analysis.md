This month's reading is displacement, at low confidence. That verdict rests on real, specific deterioration in AI-exposed industries, not on the absence of good news elsewhere, but most of the corroborating signs you would expect to see if displacement were actually underway are not showing up yet. This is the second reversal in two months: June's data pointed to displacement, July's pointed to augmentation, and August's now points back to displacement. That back-and-forth is itself worth noting: the two verdicts are close enough at these margins that small monthly moves can flip the call, so this month's answer should be read as a directional lean, not a settled regime.

The core evidence is the jobs panel comparing AI-exposed industries to a set of control industries. Exposed industries shrank 1.47% over the year to August, while control industries grew 1.4%. The gap between them has now widened for three straight readings: from roughly -2.2 points two months ago, to -2.35 in July, to -2.87 in August, past the level the dashboard treats as worth flagging. Because the control industries are growing normally, and because economy-wide numbers back this up too — nonfarm payrolls added 162,000 jobs in August and unemployment sat at 4.1%, both modest and unremarkable — this looks like something specific happening to AI-exposed work rather than a broad slowdown. That control comparison is doing real work here: without it, the exposed-industry weakness could just as easily be generic economic softness.

There is a built-in limit to what this jobs panel alone can tell you, though. A genuinely healthy story — workers in AI-exposed roles moving smoothly into other work as their old jobs shrink — would produce an identical negative number here as a story where those same workers can't find new work at all. The panel can tell you something specific is happening in exposed industries; it cannot by itself tell you whether that something is a bad landing or a fine one.

That distinction is supposed to come from the reabsorption panel, which tracks how prime-age workers (ages 25 to 54) are doing in the labor market overall. This month it crossed from flat into a caution zone for the first time in this run: the share of that group employed fell 0.3 percentage point over the past year, where it had been holding roughly steady. It's worth being precise about how much that means: this employment rate is still 0.45 point above where it stood before COVID, so this is fraying at the edges of a strong labor market, not the floor giving way.

Underneath that headline, the more detailed reabsorption indicators are not painting a picture of workers being pushed out and left stranded. Layoffs are below their normal level and still falling, while hiring has stayed flat. Falling layoffs paired with flat hiring is the signature of a frozen hiring market — employers not replacing people who leave voluntarily, rather than actively cutting staff — which is a milder story than the word displacement usually implies. Long-term unemployment ticked up only slightly, and a broader measure of underemployment (people working part-time who want full-time work, combined with the unemployed) actually improved over the year. Neither of those looks like people landing badly after losing exposed-industry jobs.

Pay tells an ambiguous story that shouldn't be over-read. Pay in AI-exposed industries is still growing faster than in control industries — by 0.81 percentage points in August, down slightly from 1.0 point in July. But because employment in exposed industries is shrinking, this number cannot distinguish a real pay story from a mechanical one: if junior, lower-paid workers are the ones leaving those industries, average pay among who's left rises automatically, with or without any change in what any individual worker earns. A separate, composition-proof check — the pay premium people get when they voluntarily switch jobs, measured across the whole economy — sits at an ordinary level, which argues against people being forced into worse-paid work broadly. That check covers all industries, not just AI-exposed ones, so it rules out one specific bad scenario without confirming or denying the exposed-industry story either way.

Job postings supply the clearest reason for caution. Postings for knowledge-work occupations, the closest match to AI-exposed work, sit about 23% below their pre-pandemic level, while postings for hands-on occupations sit about 5% above theirs. That gap is real and occupation-specific, but most of it opened up in 2022 during a rate-driven hiring pullback and has been flat since, so quoting the level as news would be misleading. What is new: over the past six months, that gap has narrowed by about 3 points — a small rotation back toward exposed-occupation postings. Postings usually move ahead of actual employment, so a leading indicator ticking the opposite direction from this month's verdict is the single strongest thing arguing against it, even though the move is small next to the 28-point gap still standing.

Two pieces of context round this out without changing the verdict. Bond markets show no sign of pricing an ordinary recession — the yield curve is not inverted — consistent with the exposed-industry weakness being its own story rather than a broad downturn. And the share of firms reporting AI use keeps climbing, from about 17% last November to 22% now, more concentrated in larger firms. That establishes AI adoption as real and growing, which makes an AI-driven explanation plausible, but it is a firm-adoption number, not a layoff number, and cannot by itself confirm anything happened to a specific worker.

Put together: two panels, the exposed-industry jobs gap and the reabsorption headline, are carrying this month's displacement call, and both are moving in the same worsening direction. But layoffs, underemployment, switcher pay, and now postings — the indicators that should confirm real harm to displaced workers if this were accelerating — are not confirming it. That is a thin, low-confidence case, not a confident one, and it should be read as such.
