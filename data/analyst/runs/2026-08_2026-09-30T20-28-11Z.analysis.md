The verdict is displacement: AI-exposed industries are still shedding jobs faster than the rest of the economy, and the broader signal that should catch workers landing elsewhere is not showing them land. This verdict is unchanged from last month, held at low confidence, and nothing in the panels that actually drive it moved. The only numbers that shifted this period were two economy-wide, descriptive series — the long-running decline in labor's share of national income ticked slightly less negative, and the yield curve got slightly less flat — and neither is a panel this verdict rests on.

Here is the strongest case for displacement. Employment in AI-exposed industries fell 1.47% over the past year while employment in a matched set of less-exposed control industries (construction, education, health, leisure) rose 1.4% — a gap that has been widening, not narrowing, over the last four monthly readings. On its own, that pattern could simply mean workers are moving from exposed industries into these other industries, which would be normal reallocation, not damage. The reason it's read as more than that: the prime-age employment-to-population ratio — the one measure broad enough to catch workers whether they land in construction or drop out of the labor force entirely — turned down over the past year as well, a shift that crossed this dashboard's own line for "workers may not be landing anywhere." That is what tips the call from reallocation to displacement.

Here is the strongest case against, and it should not be undersold. Inside that same reabsorption data, the actual hiring behavior looks calm: the gap between hires and quits is improving, and layoffs are not elevated — neither looks like a market that is actively shedding people right now. The freshest, most forward-looking indicator on the dashboard, the gap between postings for knowledge work and postings for hands-on work, has been flat for six months rather than widening, which argues against an accelerating displacement story. And economy-wide wage growth for people who change jobs — a measure immune to composition effects — is still comfortably positive, which is not what you'd expect if switchers were landing in worse jobs. None of these are evidence that nothing is happening; they are evidence that whatever is happening has not yet shown up as acceleration.

Two things were checked and ruled out as competing explanations rather than folded into the reading. A broad economic downturn would be expected to hit control industries too, especially construction, which is the most interest-rate-sensitive industry in the mix; control industries are instead growing at a healthy 1.4%, and the yield curve is not inverted, so a general macro slowdown does not explain the exposed-industry weakness. The 2023 media-industry strikes, sometimes cited as a distortion in this data, are now two years old relative to this reading and too stale to explain a current-month number. A remaining live possibility — that some of the "exposed industry" job losses are staffing and temp-help churn rather than a direct AI effect — could not be resolved with the data on hand and is flagged rather than settled.

One further point works against calling this augmentation instead: economy-wide productivity growth has been slowing, not accelerating, so there is no aggregate payoff visible yet to support a story where AI is mainly making workers more productive rather than replacing them.
