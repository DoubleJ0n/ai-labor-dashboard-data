The reading is displacement, and it is held with low confidence. It rests on positive evidence that things are getting worse for workers, not merely on the absence of improvement: employment among 25-to-54-year-olds is down about 1.44 million over twelve months, and the share of the unemployed who have been out of work more than six months has climbed 4.0 points to 27.3%. That is absorption failing, not absorption failing to improve. What keeps the confidence low is that the deterioration is one quarter old, the AI-specific jobs gap has stopped widening, and the fastest-moving indicator on the board is pointing the other way.

Nothing in the labor data moved this month. Every labor panel — jobs, employment rate, pay, postings, productivity — carries the same reading it carried last month, mostly through June 2026, with job postings through July. Three numbers on the board did change and none of them is a labor number. The share of firms using AI in any business function was restated at 21.5% for July, down from 21.5's earlier printing of 21.7% for the same month; that is a revision or a change in who answered the survey, not adoption falling. A macro-regime summary ticked down a tenth. One AI capability rating fell about fifteen points on a re-scored pool. None of these changes anything about what is happening to workers.

The exposed-versus-control jobs panel

Industries most exposed to AI — information, professional and business services — are shrinking outright, down 1.27% over the year. Industries AI has barely touched — construction, leisure and hospitality, education and health — are growing 1.27%. Naming the moving sides matters, because it is what rules out a general slump: in an ordinary downturn both sides fall together, and the hands-on side is adding jobs at a healthy clip. The bond market is not pricing a recession either; the yield curve is positive rather than inverted.

But this panel cannot carry the reading by itself, and I want to be exact about why. A widening gap between exposed and control industries is precisely what perfect reallocation would look like. If everyone who left an information-sector job walked into a health-care job, the exposed side would fall, the control side would rise, and the gap would open — with nobody worse off. Control industries growing at 1.27% is arithmetically consistent with them absorbing the entire outflow. The gap tells you something is specific to AI-exposed work. It does not tell you the people are stranded.

The gap has also stopped widening. It stood at -2.61 last month and -2.54 now, roughly flat across the last four readings. A gap that sits still is a standing condition, not news.

The 2021-22 hiring correction, weighed against the reading

Information and professional services over-hired badly in 2021-22 and have been unwinding ever since. This predates the current generation of AI tools, and I weigh it against the displacement reading rather than alongside it. It plausibly explains a large share of the exposed employment decline. It explains why the gap in job postings opened in 2022, which lines up with the interest-rate shock rather than with any model release. It explains much of the level of today's gap.

What it does not explain is the residual, and the residual is the reading. The correction is now roughly four years old and should be decaying, yet exposed industries are still contracting in absolute terms in mid-2026 while the rest of the economy adds jobs. And a hiring hangover in two sectors does not obviously produce a 1.4-million fall in prime-age employment across the whole economy or a four-point rise in long-term unemployment. That leftover is thin, and it is what I am calling displacement.

The absorption panel, and the honest case against it

The share of 25-to-54-year-olds with a job is 80.2%, down half a point over twelve months. Two things make that reading better than it sounds and one makes it worse.

Better: the level is still 0.25 points above where it stood in 2019. This is not a collapse; it is a good market getting less good. And the fall is not a statistical artifact of a growing population — the count of employed people itself fell by 1.44 million, so the ratio is falling because the numerator dropped.

Worse: the whole twelve-month decline happened inside the last three months. That is one quarter of evidence presented as a year of evidence. It comes from the household survey, which has had documented response problems through this period, and 2026 also carried a large one-off population revision in January. A single noisy quarter in this survey is a completely ordinary thing to see. If I am wrong about displacement, this is most likely where I am wrong, and I cannot rule it out from inside the data.

The supporting detail around it splits. Long-term unemployment at 27.3% is high in level terms and up 4.0 points over the year, but this series is genuinely volatile and a move of that size is suggestive rather than decisive. The broader underemployment measure has widened very slightly. Against that, layoffs are low at 1.1% of employment and still falling, and hiring minus quits sits on the comfortable side. Read together, low layoffs and weak hiring describe a frozen market, not a shedding one. Incumbents are not being pushed out. The pain is falling on people trying to get in — new entrants and people already unemployed. That is compatible with AI displacing work through simply not replacing leavers, which is what I think is going on if this reading is right. It is equally compatible with an ordinary late-cycle hiring freeze that has nothing to do with AI. I cannot separate those two from here.

The strongest thing arguing against displacement

Job postings. Advertised openings for knowledge work sit 23.1% below their February 2020 level, while postings for hands-on work sit 5.3% above theirs. That is a large gap and it looks damning until you read the direction: over the last six months the knowledge-work side has been recovering, closing about 2.6 points of the gap. The level is largely a 2022 interest-rate event still sitting in the data; the change is the news, and the change is on the improving side.

This cuts hard against the displacement reading for two reasons. Postings lead actual hiring, so this panel should turn before the employment numbers do. And it is cut by type of work rather than by industry, which is a better way of identifying who is actually exposed to AI than counting everyone employed in the information sector, janitors included. So the indicator most likely to be right first, on the better measure of exposure, says demand for exposed work is stabilising or slightly improving. It is also fresher — July, against June for everything supporting the displacement reading. The counter-signal is not stale; the supporting evidence is the older material. This is the single biggest reason the reading is low confidence rather than moderate.

I am not treating strong hands-on postings as evidence of absorption. Demand rotating between kinds of work is not the same as workers rotating with it, and a job advertisement is an advertised intention, not a hire.

Pay, and why I am not using it

Pay in AI-exposed industries is growing 4.63% a year against 3.53% in barely-touched industries, and that gap widened this month. On its face that is backwards from displacement. I am not counting it for either side, because there is a mechanical explanation that fits too well. Employment in those industries is falling. If junior hiring in information and professional services has stopped, the people left are more senior on average, and the average wage rises with nobody getting a raise.

The check on this points the same way. A separate measure that follows the same individuals twelve months apart shows people who stayed in their jobs getting 3.6% and people who switched getting 4.4%, neither accelerating, while the industry average jumped four-tenths of a point in a single month. Averages moving when the people underneath them change is exactly what that pattern looks like. The pay panel is unresolved, not supportive.

That same individual-level tracker does carry one genuine piece of counter-evidence: job switchers are still being paid a premium of about 0.8 points over stayers, and that premium has been recovering. People who move are being bid for. Two limits on it. It is economy-wide, covering every worker in the country, so it cannot say anything specific about workers leaving AI-exposed jobs — a different and much smaller group. And it is a median, so a bad outcome concentrated in a minority is precisely the shape it would miss.

Productivity

Output per hour is running at 2.8% a year, up from 2.5%, and briefly inside the band the late-1990s internet boom produced. If AI were making workers more valuable rather than redundant, gains showing up in productivity is what that would look like, and this is the most substantive point on the augmentation side of the board. Three things hold me back from leaning on it. It is economy-wide and cannot attribute anything to AI-exposed work. It is measured through March 2026, a quarter older than the jobs and employment figures, so it cannot yet reflect a turn in either direction. And output per hour rises arithmetically when hours fall faster than output — which is also what a displacement episode produces. One good quarter is not a break in trend.

Worth noting alongside it: labor's share of national income is 51.0% and has fallen four quarters running, now below its previous record low. That share has been drifting down for forty years for reasons that have nothing to do with AI, and it typically drops in recessions, so I count it as zero evidence. It is evocative rather than probative — productivity elevated while labor's slice shrinks unusually fast is the shape gains not reaching workers would take.

Alternatives I considered and rejected

A general recession. Fails immediately: the un-exposed side of the economy is adding jobs at 1.27% a year and its postings are above pre-pandemic levels. A general slump pulls both sides down.

The 2022 interest-rate shock as the whole story. This is the strongest rival and I gave it real weight, as described above. It explains the level of the postings gap and much of the exposed jobs decline. It does not explain why prime-age employment is falling now, in mid-2026, with the yield curve positive and the untouched industries growing. Mixed evidence is not the same as a confounded reading, so I did not convert it into a non-answer.

Augmentation. The affirmative case would need productivity gains plus pay rising for exposed workers. The pay leg is disqualified by the composition problem above and contradicted by the individual-level tracker. That leaves an economy-wide productivity number five months old with one quarter inside its band. Augmentation working would need positive evidence that exposed workers are being made more valuable, and there is no composition-proof, exposed-industry version of that anywhere in this data. So augmentation would have to rest on the absence of anything bad happening — and something bad is happening in the employment numbers, so it cannot rest even there.

News

Coverage of the Iran conflict is too recent to be in June data, and a war-driven demand shock would hit construction and hospitality at least as hard as information and professional services, which is the opposite of the pattern here. Federal Reserve and mortgage coverage is the live version of the interest-rate story and is already discounted in how I read the postings panel. The monthly payrolls report showed 158,984,000 jobs, up 57,000, with unemployment at 4.2% — economy-wide, from the establishment survey, so it cannot speak to exposed industries specifically and cannot be netted against the household survey's prime-age figure. What it does do is make the "untouched industries are booming and absorbing everyone" story less comfortable than the 1.27% growth figure alone suggests: total hiring across the economy is barely positive. A Federal Reserve Bank of New York piece attributing hiring weakness to AI is a hypothesis to test against the series, not corroboration of them, and it generated no test I was not already running.

What would show this reading is wrong

If the prime-age employment rate stops falling year on year — the twelve-month change coming back to -0.1 or better — while the exposed-versus-control jobs gap also pulls back to -2.2 or narrower, then the people leaving exposed work were being absorbed all along and the last quarter was noise. Both series publish monthly and will produce two or three new readings before early November, so this will get tested. What would push the reading the other way, toward higher confidence in displacement, is the knowledge-work postings gap resuming its widening, or a second consecutive quarter of falling prime-age employment confirming that the last one was real.
