The displacement reading holds this month, and it rests on actual measured cooling, not just the absence of good news. Jobs in industries most exposed to AI, information, professional and business services, and financial activities, fell 1.47% over the past year. Jobs in industries AI has barely touched, construction, leisure and hospitality, education and health, rose 1.4% over the same period. That gap has widened for four straight monthly readings, which is a real trend rather than a one-month blip. Separately, and covering a different population, the share of 25- to 54-year-olds holding any job, an economy-wide figure across all industries, slipped 0.3 percentage point over the past year. That is measured deterioration, not silence. It is also modest: the level itself, 80.4%, still sits slightly above where it stood before the pandemic, so this reads as a cooling labor market rather than a collapsing one.

Nothing moved substantively since last month's read of the same August data. The one panel that changed at all is the share of firms reporting AI use in any business function, which rose to 23.2% of firms from 22.4%, continuing a steady climb from 17.3% ten months ago and concentrated more heavily among large employers (38.4% of firms with 250 or more employees, versus 23.6% of the smallest). That tells us AI deployment is real and growing. It does not, by itself, link any specific firm's AI use to a layoff decision, and it did not move the verdict.

Three non-AI explanations for the exposed-industry weakness were checked and none holds up. Interest-rate sensitivity in financial activities is plausible in principle, but construction, one of the most rate-sensitive industries in the economy, sits in the control group and is growing, which argues against a pure-rate story explaining the exposed-side weakness. The 2023 media and entertainment strikes are three years in the past and too old to be driving an August 2026 reading. Staffing services acting as a leading indicator in "any" slowdown is a fair general point, but it is not itself evidence that a slowdown is currently underway. None of these fit the pattern in the concurrent control-industry and national data, so this remains a direct displacement reading rather than one explained away by something else, held at low confidence.

The strongest thing arguing against the verdict is the shape of how workers appear to be leaving exposed industries. Layoffs are low and still falling, not elevated. Hiring is neither speeding up nor slowing beyond its ordinary range. That combination looks like a hiring freeze, employers not backfilling roles that automation may have made unnecessary, rather than an active push to shed workers. That is a real but meaningfully weaker claim than mass layoffs would be. More important still: job postings, usually the earliest panel to move when a slowdown accelerates, have been flat for six months. Postings for knowledge-heavy work sit about 23% below their pre-pandemic level; postings for hands-on work sit about 5% above. That gap opened back in 2022, mostly for interest-rate reasons that predate the current wave of AI deployment, and it has not widened further since. If AI-driven displacement were accelerating right now, this is the indicator that should show it first, and it has not.

Two numbers worth flagging as inconclusive rather than supportive either way. Pay in exposed industries is growing faster than in control industries, 4.0% versus 3.19% over the past year, but exposed-industry employment is shrinking at the same time. A shrinking industry's average pay rises mechanically as its lowest-paid jobs disappear first, so this number cannot be read as evidence that AI is making surviving exposed workers more valuable; it is genuinely ambiguous this month. Separately, economy-wide productivity growth slowed to 2.2% in the year through this June, from 2.9% previously. It remains above its longer-run average but has dropped out of the range associated with a genuine AI productivity boom, so there is no augmentation dividend visible in this quarter's data to weigh against the displacement signal.

Background, not evidence either way: labor's share of national income fell 1.3 points over the past year, one of the fastest declines on record, but this is a forty-year secular trend with no way to attribute it to AI specifically, and it covers the whole economy, not exposed industries. The national payroll report for the same month showed the aggregate economy still adding jobs (162,000) with unemployment at 4.1%, but that figure covers all industries and all ages in a single month, a different population and a different survey than the prime-age, twelve-month figure discussed above; it is used here only to confirm this isn't a broad recession, not compared directly against the exposed-industry numbers. AI capability benchmarks (task-completion scores, expert-comparison ratings) were also reviewed and excluded: they measure performance on clean, scored tasks, not any employer's actual decision to change staffing, so they cannot move this reading in either direction.

What would undercut this reading: over the next three months, the prime-age employment rate's 12-month change would need to recover to roughly flat or better while control-industry job growth stays at 1% a year or above, showing the broader economy actively absorbing people leaving exposed industries rather than simply not yet punishing them.
