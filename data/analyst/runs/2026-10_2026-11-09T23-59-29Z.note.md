The reading this month is AUGMENTATION: the data still describes displacement that hasn't started, rather than augmentation that's visibly working. That distinction matters because this verdict rests on the absence of deterioration — on hiring and employment holding roughly steady — not on any positive sign that AI is making exposed workers more productive or better paid. Confidence is low.

Nothing that actually decides this verdict moved since last month. The share of firms using AI in any business function ticked up to 24.2% from 23.8%, and the bond market eased slightly, but both are context, not evidence either way. Every panel that bears on displacement versus augmentation — employment in exposed versus unexposed industries, the prime-age employment rate, pay comparisons, job postings, worker income share — is unchanged from a month ago. This is a quiet month on the evidence that matters.

The strongest case for the verdict: the share of prime-age adults (25-54) who are employed has been flat over the past year, but it still sits comfortably above its pre-pandemic level, and layoffs are running below their historical norm. Soft hiring paired with low layoffs looks like a frozen market, not one where exposed workers are being pushed out. And industries AI should mostly leave alone — construction, hospitality, education, health care — are adding jobs at 1.17% a year, above their own normal pace, which rules out a general downturn as the explanation for weakness anywhere else.

The strongest case against: employment in AI-exposed industries is falling, down 1.35% over the past year against that same 1.17% growth elsewhere — about a 2.5-point gap that has now sat past the alarm level for three straight months without closing. It isn't widening, but it isn't healing either; it's parked. A second, independent dataset backs this up with a different cut of the labor market: job postings for knowledge-work occupations sit 21.6% below their pre-COVID level, while postings for hands-on occupations sit 8.1% above theirs. Two different data sources agree something specific to exposed work is happening. The usual explanation is that most of this opened during the 2022 interest-rate shock and has simply never closed, rather than being new damage — but a gap this persistent, years later, and overlapping a year of fast-rising AI adoption, is also what it would look like if an old shock were being locked in by something newer. That possibility cannot be ruled out here. Separately, the share of long-term unemployed workers is rising, both over the past year and faster still over the past three months — a genuine warning sign, even though it covers the whole economy rather than exposed industries alone.

What to watch: the share of long-term unemployed in the reabsorption data, since it is the fastest-moving signal of whether people leaving exposed industries are actually landing new jobs, and it is currently the one piece of that picture moving the wrong way.
