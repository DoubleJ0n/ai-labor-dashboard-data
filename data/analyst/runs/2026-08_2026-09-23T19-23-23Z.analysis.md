The reading stays at displacement. That means the data continue to show AI-exposed industries losing ground relative to the rest of the economy, not merely failing to gain. This is not a call built on the absence of bad news — it rests on two real, currently-crossed markers: exposed-industry employment is falling in a way that does not show up in comparable non-exposed industries, and the broader gauge of whether displaced workers are finding other work has itself turned down past the point the dashboard treats as a warning line. Confidence in the call is low, for reasons explained below.

What's new this month: very little. Of the eleven panels that feed this verdict, nine are completely unchanged from last month's data — the employment divergence between exposed and non-exposed industries, the wage comparison, the prime-age employment rate, worker share of income, AI adoption, and the macro backdrop are all reading exactly what they read a month ago. The only two things that moved were minor and not load-bearing: the gap between job postings for AI-exposed occupations (things like writing, coding, analysis) and postings for hands-on occupations (construction, repair, healthcare support) widened slightly, from 27.9 percentage points to 28.8, continuing a level that has mostly sat still since a 2022 hiring slowdown rather than reflecting anything new; and a benchmark score comparing AI model outputs to human expert work — used here only as background on how capable the tools are, not as evidence anyone lost a job — ticked down slightly. Neither changes the picture.

The strongest evidence for displacement: employment in AI-exposed industries (information, professional and business services, financial activities) fell 1.47% over the past year, while employment in a comparison set of industries less exposed to AI (construction, education, healthcare) rose 1.4% over the same period. Those are different groups of workers, which is exactly the point — a broad economic slowdown would normally hit both, and it isn't. Alongside that, the share of prime-age adults (25 to 54) who are employed — an economy-wide measure covering all workers, not just those in exposed industries — has fallen 0.3 percentage point over the past year, which is enough to cross the level the dashboard treats as showing genuine cooling in how well displaced workers are being reabsorbed elsewhere. On its own that move is not large — the employment rate itself remains slightly above its pre-pandemic level — but it is a real deterioration, not a pause in improvement.

The strongest evidence complicating the case: the way exposed industries are shrinking looks like a freeze, not a flood. Within those industries, layoffs are currently running below their normal rate, and the balance between hiring and quitting is essentially flat rather than showing the pattern you'd expect if employers were actively pushing people out. That combination — weak hiring, low layoffs — points toward exposed employers simply not adding or replacing jobs, rather than shedding staff outright. It is still a mechanism consistent with AI substituting for new hires, but it is a slower, quieter process than mass displacement, and it means the burden is likely falling on people trying to enter these industries rather than people already in them. On top of that, the earliest-moving indicator here — the gap between job postings in exposed versus hands-on occupations — has stayed essentially flat for six months rather than widening further, which argues the damage in that channel already happened and is not currently accelerating. This is the single most important caveat: if displacement were actively intensifying, this is the panel that should be moving first, and it isn't.

Other panels were checked and set aside rather than used as evidence either way. Pay in AI-exposed industries is rising slightly faster than in non-exposed industries, which sounds like a case against displacement, but exposed employment is shrinking at the same time — so the raw pay gap is exactly what you'd expect if lower-paid or newer workers are the ones leaving, regardless of whether AI is the cause. A cleaner check that follows the same workers over time — the premium people earn by switching jobs versus staying put, which does not have this composition problem — shows switching still pays more, but by less than its historical average, which neither confirms nor rules out a genuine pay story. Economy-wide productivity growth slowed to 2.2% from 2.9%, below the range you'd expect if AI were delivering broad efficiency gains — this argues against an augmentation story but is not itself evidence of displacement, since it covers all workers, not just those in exposed industries. Labor's overall share of national income is falling at an unusually fast clip, but that decline predates AI by decades and past recessions produced faster drops, so it's read as a long-running trend rather than new evidence. AI adoption by firms continues to rise and is more concentrated at large employers, which removes any argument that there isn't enough real-world AI use yet to matter, but adoption alone doesn't show that anyone specifically lost work because of it.

A plausible alternative story — that this is an ordinary cyclical or interest-rate-driven slowdown in exposed sectors like finance, rather than anything to do with AI — was tested and not adopted. The comparison industries are healthy, the yield curve is not signaling a downturn, and the broader economy added jobs and held a low unemployment rate this period. None of that rules out AI as the explanation, but it also means no competing cause currently has enough support of its own to override the displacement reading.

What to watch: the prime-age employment-population ratio in the reabsorption panel. If its year-over-year change turns back toward flat or positive while job growth in non-exposed industries stays solid, that would be the clearest sign that workers leaving AI-exposed industries are being reabsorbed rather than left behind, and this reading would reverse.
