Notification: New results are in: displacement verdict as AI-exposed industries shed jobs while others hire
Falsifier: The prime-age employment-rate chart moves first — if its 12-month change climbs back to -0.2 or better while the exposed-minus-control jobs gap narrows to -1.75 or above by late October, then what I am calling displacement was reallocation plus the tail of the 2021-22 hiring correction.

---

The reading is displacement, early and only moderately supported. It rests on deterioration that is actually visible rather than on the absence of bad news: the share of 25-to-54-year-olds with a job is falling, unemployment spells are getting longer, and the industries most exposed to AI are the ones shedding jobs.

The strongest evidence is where the job losses sit. Employment in information, professional and business services, and finance — where AI can do the most of the work — is down 1.3% over the past year. Employment in construction, leisure and hospitality, and education and health, where AI touches very little of the day, is up 1.3%. Both sides are moving, in opposite directions. That matters because it rules out a general slowdown: if the whole economy were cooling, construction and health care would be cooling too, and instead their pay is growing 3.5% a year and their job postings sit about 4% above where they were in February 2020. Unemployment overall is 4.2%, payrolls added 57,000 in the latest month, and bond markets are not pricing a downturn. Whatever is pulling exposed employment down is specific to those industries.

The problem is that the same split is exactly what successful reallocation looks like. If a hundred jobs move from information to health care, exposed employment falls, other employment rises, and nobody is displaced. So the question is whether people leaving are landing. Two signals say landing has got harder. The share of 25-to-54-year-olds in work is 80.2%, down half a point over the year — and the entire decline arrived in the last quarter, after nine roughly flat months. The share of unemployed people out of work more than six months is 27.3%, up 4 points over the year.

Both of those come with honest caveats that keep this call moderate rather than strong. That 80.2% is still about a quarter of a point above its 2019 level, near the top of its historical range, and a half-point annual fall is close to an ordinary year in the 2010s given how much this series normally moves. The long-term unemployment share can also rise for a harmless reason: short spells ending faster while long spells hold steady.

The strongest case against the displacement reading is three findings, none of them older than the evidence supporting it.

Job postings. Postings for knowledge work sit about 24% below their February 2020 level, while postings for hands-on work sit about 4% above theirs. That is a large hole — but it is filling in, narrowing by 3 points over six months and well off its worst point in late 2023. Postings lead hiring, so a recovering postings series argues the worst is behind. This is the most recent evidence available, which means the strongest counterweight here is newer than the case for displacement, not older.

Pay. Wages in the AI-heavy industries are growing 4.6% a year against 3.5% where AI has barely arrived. Faster pay growth where AI is heaviest is backwards from a story about those workers being replaced. I am not treating it as evidence of augmentation, for one reason: employment in those industries is shrinking about 1.3%, and when a workforce shrinks the average wage rises mechanically as lower-paid people leave, with nobody getting a raise. But my reading runs against the direction of this evidence, and a reader who thinks that composition effect is small should treat the pay gap as real evidence that AI is making these workers more valuable.

Hiring flows. Hiring runs at 3.3% of employment a month, voluntary quitting at 1.9%, and that margin has improved slightly over the year. Both legs are low, though: few people hired, few choosing to leave. The net looks healthy partly because quitting is subdued, not because hiring is strong.

There is a full non-AI explanation with real evidence behind it. Information, professional services and finance over-hired in 2021 and 2022 and have been unwinding ever since. That explains the worst of the sectoral gap in late 2023, the long stretch of weak knowledge-work postings, and the low-churn market. What it explains less well is why exposed employment is still outright falling four to five years in. A hiring correction is a one-time adjustment to a level — employment should flatten out lower, not keep declining after postings, the correction's own leading indicator, have already turned up. That unexplained remainder is why this is not filed as an unresolvable confound. The hiring correction carries more than half of what we can see. I do not think it carries all of it.

Two figures often cited here cover every worker in the country, not the exposed industries, and cannot be used as evidence about them. Output per hour is growing 2.8% a year, the fastest since the internet boom. Labour's share of all income generated in the economy is 51.0%, the lowest in records going back to 1947, down a full point over four quarters against a long-run drift of about a tenth of a point a year. Production is getting more efficient, the gains are not reaching wages, and it is happening without a recession — but rising output per hour is equally the signature of workers being made more valuable, so on its own it settles nothing.

Watch the prime-age employment rate. If the drop that arrived in the last quarter reverses, the foundation of this displacement reading goes with it.
