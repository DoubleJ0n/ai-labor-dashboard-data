The reading this month is augmentation, and the honest description of what is behind it is this: the damage that displacement would cause has not shown up in the aggregate labour market yet. That is not the same thing as evidence that AI is making workers more valuable. Almost nothing on this dashboard shows workers being lifted; what it shows is an outflow from AI-exposed industries that so far appears to be landing somewhere. The reading is directional and it is held with low confidence.

What is new since the last analysis: essentially nothing. One figure changed. The survey of how many firms use AI in any business function revised its July reading from 21.5% of firms to 21.8%. Both numbers are dated the same month, so this is a correction to an observation already published rather than a fresh one. Every other panel carries the identical number it carried last time. A month in which nothing moved does not license a new story, so most of what follows is a restatement of a picture that has stayed put.

Start with where something is clearly happening. In the industries most exposed to AI — information, professional and business services, financial activities — employment is not merely growing slowly, it is shrinking, down 1.19% against a year ago. In the comparison industries that AI has barely touched — construction, leisure and hospitality, and similar hands-on work — employment is up 1.16%. Job postings tell the same story cut a different way, by occupation rather than industry: postings for knowledge work sit about 24% below where they were in February 2020, while postings for hands-on work sit about 5% above theirs. Six years on, knowledge-work demand has not repaired.

That divergence is real, but it can only tell you that something is happening specifically to exposed work. It cannot tell you whether those jobs were destroyed or whether the people in them moved. Perfect reallocation — every displaced worker re-employed elsewhere the next week — would produce exactly the same picture on those panels. So the question moves to whether people are landing.

On that, the central measure is the share of 25-54-year-olds who have a job. It is 80.4%, unchanged over twelve months, and still about half a point above where it stood in 2019. Alongside it, layoffs are low and falling. Firms hired at a rate of 3.4% of employment in the latest month against voluntary quits of 2.0%, and that gap has widened slightly over the year because hiring is running ahead of quitting, not because people stopped quitting. Workers who change jobs are still paid a premium for changing: switchers' pay is growing 4.4% against 3.6% for people who stayed put, and that premium has been recovering, not eroding. That last figure is economy-wide, covering all workers rather than only those in AI-exposed industries, so it cannot establish anything about exposed workers specifically. It does say that the general machinery of moving between jobs is still paying people who use it.

I also tested whether this is simply a slowdown wearing an AI mask. A general downturn drags everything down together. It has not: the comparison industries are growing, hands-on postings are above their pre-COVID level, layoffs are declining, and the bond market has an uninverted yield curve with expected inflation near 2.3%. I do not lean on the yield curve to excuse weakness already visible in the data — it prices expectations, not history — but the contemporaneous test on the healthy side of the labour market is the one that matters, and the cyclical story fails it. A rate-driven story that spares construction, the most interest-rate-sensitive industry there is, and hits information instead, is not a rate story.

Other explanations I considered and set aside. A federal contracting drawdown would land squarely in professional and business services and would look like this — but there is no series here that measures federal contracting, so naming it would be pointing at something I cannot see. Immigration policy shifting the size of the working-age population is likewise unmeasurable here, and the employment share is a ratio, so it is largely insulated from population size anyway. The Hollywood strikes explain part of the historical shape of this gap in 2023; they are not available for 2026. And the widely-cited finding that AI chat usage splits roughly 57% augmentation to 43% automation is nearly a year stale, is one vendor's sample of conversations, and conversations are not jobs. I did not count it, and the reading would be the same if it read the other way round.

Now the case against, which is substantial.

The most uncomfortable combination on this dashboard is exposed-industry employment contracting 1.19% while economy-wide layoffs are falling. Put those together and the exposed jobs are mostly disappearing through people leaving and not being replaced, rather than through anyone being dismissed. That is a frozen market rather than a shedding one, and the harm in a frozen market falls on people who never got hired — new graduates, career changers, re-entrants. The prime-age employment ratio measures whether people who already have jobs keep them. It is close to blind to the people who never got in. If displacement is running here, that is the shape it has, and this dashboard is poorly placed to see it.

There is partial corroboration for exactly that. The share of the unemployed who have been out of work more than six months is 25.5%, up 0.6 points over the year — still within normal range, but moving the wrong way. And the gap between the broad underemployment rate and the headline unemployment rate has widened to 3.8 points, up 0.2 over the year. That gap is the measure that widens when people land in part-time or marginal work while the headline job count holds — which is precisely what a displacement episode absorbed into worse work would look like. It is the most deteriorated single readout here. The shorter three-month window on that measure disagrees, which is why I do not weight it more, but I am not going to bury it either.

The employment ratio itself has a shorter-run wobble the headline hides: over the last three months it is down 0.3 points, which is a large move for this series and close to the point at which I would treat it as a turn rather than noise. It is flat over twelve months and down over three. If that shorter window is the truth and the annual figure is lagging, this reading is wrong.

One measurement note that a reader relying on the employment panel deserves. The underlying components of that panel show both employed people and the working-age population lower than a year ago, which reads oddly next to a flat employment share. That is an artefact of the annual January re-basing of population controls in the household survey, which moves both legs by amounts far larger than a normal month and does so on a schedule that has nothing to do with the economy. The ratio is the reliable number and the component levels this year are not quotable. The panel corrects an earlier version of its own note on this point, which is to its credit, but it means the decomposition can only be used as a direction check.

Then there is labour's share of national income, at 51.0%. It has fallen for four straight quarters, is down a full percentage point over the year against a long-run drift of roughly a tenth of a point a year, and is the lowest reading in a series that runs back to 1947. A fall that fast without a recession is what you would expect if capital were substituting for labour. Three things stop it moving the verdict: it covers the whole economy, so it can never be evidence about AI-exposed work whatever it does; it has been drifting down for forty years for reasons that predate AI entirely; and ordinary recessions produced faster falls still, in 2009 and 2010. It is also the oldest evidence in the run, dated March, five months behind the job and posting numbers. So the strongest counter-evidence is also the least attributable and the least current — but it is a record low, and it is falling at a pace this series has only exceeded in downturns.

The productivity panel is supposed to be the test of whether the gains an augmentation story predicts are actually showing up. Economy-wide productivity growth is 2.2%, better than the 2010s average of about 1.3%, but down from 2.9% and now below the range the panel treats as internet-boom-like. It fails to corroborate the augmentation reading rather than supporting it, and it is economy-wide in any case.

Two more things I read and did not count. Pay in AI-exposed industries is growing 4.22% against 3.21% in the comparison industries, which superficially looks like the opposite of displacement. But exposed employment is falling, and when the group is shrinking from the bottom — junior hiring stopping first — the average rises with nobody getting a raise. Genuine pay strength and pure composition predict the same number here, and I cannot separate them, so I report it and do not rely on it. And the AI capability benchmarks — task-length horizons, model-versus-model ratings, model-versus-expert win rates — say nothing about any worker. A model beating an expert on a scored task and an employer deciding to stop paying that expert are different events, and only the second one is labour evidence.

On the news: the latest employment report showed payrolls falling 23,000 on the month with unemployment at 4.1%. It is one month, from a different survey, covering all ages, against a twelve-month prime-age absorption measure — three things differ at once, and netting them is a mistake. It is also fully consistent with the frozen-market picture already drawn from the hiring and layoff data. It does not move the reading, but it is a live warning that absorption may be about to weaken, and a large downward revision to prior months would be a genuine reason to revisit.

One further fragility worth stating plainly: the modest improvement in the exposed-versus-control jobs gap over the last month sits entirely inside the two most recent payroll months, which are preliminary and get revised most, and the statistical model used to estimate new and closing businesses tends to undercount job losses right as a downturn begins. If that improvement revises away, the gap is at the level I would treat as a genuine break.

What would overturn the augmentation reading: the share of 25-54-year-olds with a job falling close to a full point over twelve months while the comparison industries are still adding jobs. The requirement that the healthy industries stay healthy is deliberate — if everything falls together, that is an ordinary recession, not an AI-specific absorption failure. Both the panels involved are monthly and will publish three more observations before the end of the window, so the test will be settled on fresh data rather than stale.
