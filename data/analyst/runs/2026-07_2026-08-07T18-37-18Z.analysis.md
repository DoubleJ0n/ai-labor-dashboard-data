The reading this month is augmentation, and it has flipped from displacement. Before anything else: this is not a finding that AI is visibly making workers more valuable. It is a finding that the places where displacement would have to show up — people losing work and not finding new work — still do not show it, while the one AI-specific measure that moved, moved slightly the right way. Confidence is low. The honest description is "displacement has not appeared in the absorption data," not "augmentation is demonstrably working."

What moved since last month

Four numbers changed. Jobs in AI-exposed industries relative to control industries improved slightly: exposed industries are shrinking at 1.19% a year while control industries add jobs at 1.16% a year, so the gap between them is 2.35 percentage points, narrower than the 2.54 points a month ago. Naming the sides matters here: the improvement came from the exposed side shrinking a little less, not from control racing ahead. Productivity for the whole economy fell to 2.2% growth a year from 2.9%. Pay growth in exposed industries relative to control narrowed a touch, to 1.00 points from 1.11. And a benchmark of AI model quality ticked up fractionally.

Everything else on the board is unchanged from last month, including the panel the reading actually rests on.

Why the reading is augmentation

The decisive measure is the share of 25-to-54-year-olds who have a job. It is 80.4%, unchanged from a year ago, and about 0.45 points above where it sat in 2019 before the pandemic. If AI were pushing people out of exposed work faster than the rest of the economy could take them in, this is the number that would sag, and it has not.

Around it, the layoff rate is 1.1% of employment per month, low by historical standards and lower than a year ago. Employers are not shedding people. The share of the unemployed who have been out of work more than six months is 25.5%, up 0.6 points over the year — rising, but only barely, which means people who lose jobs are still being caught roughly as fast as before. Economy-wide, people who change employers are still landing at a pay premium over people who stay put, 4.4% against 3.6%, and that premium has been recovering over recent readings. Job-changers getting paid more is what reallocation with decent landings looks like; it is not what an economy looks like when a category of work is being removed.

On the AI-specific side, the gap between online job postings for knowledge work and postings for hands-on work narrowed by 2.6 points over six months.

The cyclical alternative, and why it does not fully clear

The obvious rival explanation for shrinking employment in information, professional services and finance is not AI at all — it is interest rates. The 10-year yield is 2.4% above expected inflation, which is restrictive. If that were the whole story, though, you would expect the control industries to be struggling too, and construction, the most rate-sensitive industry there is, sits inside the control group and is not dragging it negative; control industries are still adding jobs. The yield curve is not inverted. So the weakness is specific to the exposed industries rather than general.

That test is not clean, and I want to be precise about where it fails. Real estate and insurance sit inside the exposed group and are themselves rate-sensitive. Temporary help sits inside professional and business services, and temp help turns down first in any slowdown regardless of cause. Professional services also carries heavy federal contracting exposure. A rate squeeze plus a contracting squeeze plus a temp-help unwind could produce a 1.19% contraction with no AI in it at all. Nothing in this data separates those from an AI effect.

The 2021-22 over-hiring story, and the residual it leaves

The standard benign explanation for the exposed-industry gap is that these industries hired far too many people in 2021 and 2022 and have been giving those jobs back ever since. That explains the deepest part of the trough well — the widest the gap ever got was in late 2023, which was also when the writers' and actors' strikes shut down large parts of the information sector.

It explains the present much less well. We are more than four years past the hiring peak. An unwind should be finished, or at least still closing. Instead exposed industries are not merely growing more slowly than the rest of the economy, they are contracting outright, and job postings for knowledge work sit 23% below where they were in February 2020 with no recovery, while postings for hands-on work sit 5% above theirs. That residual — the part the hiring correction does not account for — is what keeps a displacement story alive, and it is the main reason confidence here is low rather than moderate.

The postings improvement has a hole in it. The 2.6-point narrowing over six months is a change in the gap, and I cannot see from this data which side produced it. Knowledge-work postings recovering and hands-on postings weakening would both narrow the gap by the same amount, and only one of those is good news. I am not treating that narrowing as strong support.

The frozen market

The hiring rate is 3.4% of employment a month and the quit rate is 2.0%. The gap between hiring and quitting improved over the year, but it improved because quitting fell, not because hiring rose. Hiring at 3.4% is low by historical standards. Layoffs low plus hiring low is a frozen market rather than a shedding one: incumbents keep their jobs, and the cost falls on people trying to get in.

That shape is genuinely ambiguous. It is exactly what a slow AI displacement would look like in its early years, where nobody is fired but nobody is hired either. It is also exactly what a restrictive-rates, high-uncertainty economy produces. I cannot tell them apart, and I say so rather than counting the improvement as strength.

The number that cuts hardest against this reading

The share of national income going to workers rather than to capital is 51.0%. That is below the previous record low in the data, set in December 2025. It has fallen four quarters running, down about 1.0 point over the year, which is roughly seven times the pace of the slow secular decline that has run for forty years and puts this among the fastest declines on record.

That is the most alarming figure on the board and it points the other way from the verdict. Two things stop it from moving the reading, and neither is a dismissal. First, it covers every industry in the economy, so it cannot tell AI-exposed work apart from anything else — while the employment and postings figures above are specific to exposed industries, and those are different groups of workers. Second, its own history shows ordinary recessions producing faster falls than this one: 2.2 points in 2009, 1.6 in 2010. A decline at this speed is within what the business cycle produces with no technology story attached. It is also the stalest number here, from March, so it predates the July labour data this reading is built on. It is recorded as a live worry, not a vote.

Two smaller pieces of bad news deserve naming. The gap between the broad underemployment rate and the headline unemployment rate is 3.8 points, up 0.2 over the year and the most deteriorated single reading anywhere on this board — that is people working part-time who want full-time work and people who have given up looking. Its three-month direction is the other way, improving by 0.1, so the short and long horizons disagree. And the year-over-year direction of prime-age employment is down, not flat; the employment share held steady because the prime-age population fell too. A shrinking working-age population is a labour-supply story, most plausibly immigration, not a job-destruction story — but it means the flat headline is doing less work than it appears to.

Context on AI itself

The share of firms using AI in any business function is 21.5%, up from 17.3% last November and rising in seven of the last eight readings. Large firms adopt at nearly twice the rate of the smallest, so the share of workers exposed is higher than the share of firms. But this survey asks whether a tool is present somewhere in the business, not whether it replaced anyone, and it does not link a firm's adoption to that firm's headcount. Rising adoption means an AI explanation is available. It does not mean it is operating. The most useful thing to note is that adoption has climbed about four points since November while the prime-age employment share has gone nowhere.

The one survey that splits AI use into automating tasks versus assisting people reads 43% automation, 57% augmentation, unchanged. That reading is from September 2025 — eleven months old, describing the fastest-moving thing on this board. It predates every labour month here and I give it essentially no weight.

Productivity for the whole economy is growing at 2.2% a year, above the 1.3% average of the 2010s but below the 2.7-3.4% band of the late-1990s internet boom, and decelerating from 2.9%. It is consistent with a story where AI helps, and it is economy-wide, so it attributes nothing to AI-exposed work specifically. It is a weak consistency check, not support.

A note on the pay figure

Pay in exposed industries is growing 4.22% a year against 3.21% in control industries. That looks like the most augmentation-shaped number available, and I am not using it, because employment in those same industries is falling. When entry-level hiring stops, the workers left behind skew senior and the industry average rises without anyone getting a raise. A genuine pay story and a composition artefact predict the same observation, and this data cannot separate them. The economy-wide job-switcher premium argues mildly against pure composition, but it cannot be cut to exposed industries, so it cannot confirm an exposed-industry pay story either. The exposed pay gap is treated as uninformative in both directions.

What would show this reading is wrong

The prime-age employment share is the panel to watch. If its 12-month change drops to 0.7 points or worse while control industries are still adding jobs at 0.8% or better, then people leaving exposed work are not being reabsorbed elsewhere, and the augmentation reading was wrong.
