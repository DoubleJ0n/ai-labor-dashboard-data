The reading is displacement, holding for a second month. This call rests on a specific, dated change in the data — not merely on the absence of good news — and it comes with low confidence, because several other measures on the same dashboard show no comparable stress.

Almost nothing moved this month. Job postings in AI-exposed knowledge work are still down 22.9% from their February 2020 level, against a 5% gain for hands-on control occupations over the same period, but the trend over the past six months has turned mildly less negative, a small point against the displacement story deepening further. A capability benchmark for AI models (GDPval) also dropped sharply, but that was due to a change in the benchmark's own comparison pool, not any change in what AI can do or any labor-market event, so it carries no weight here.

The case for displacement rests on a change in shape. For over two years, AI-exposed industries grew jobs more slowly than comparable non-exposed industries while the broader labor market kept absorbing workers fine — consistent with people reallocating out of shrinking niches into better jobs, not losing work outright. That pattern has broken. Exposed-industry employment is now down 1.47% over the past year while comparable non-exposed industries (construction, leisure and hospitality, education and health) grew a healthy 1.4% over the same stretch. At the same time, for five straight months, the broader labor market has stopped absorbing workers as it normally would: the share of prime-age adults with a job has fallen 0.3 percentage points over the past year, past the level this dashboard treats as an early warning sign. Exposed-sector weakness and broader softening now moving together, rather than sitting beside an otherwise-fine aggregate, is why this reads as displacement rather than reallocation. Two things temper that: the prime-age employment rate remains 0.45 points above its 2019 level, so this is a softening off a strong base rather than a collapse, and the exposed-industry jobs comparison cannot by itself tell workers being laid off from workers voluntarily leaving contracting industries for better ones — a fully healthy reallocation could produce a very similar number.

The strongest evidence against displacement: a separate government survey of job openings and quits shows hiring minus quits unchanged over the past year and layoffs below their long-run norm, not what you'd expect from active shedding. And a wage-growth measure that tracks the same individuals over time shows job-switchers still earning about 0.8 percentage points more than people who stay, slightly better than normal, not the depressed premium you'd expect if switchers were landing in worse jobs. These figures are economy-wide, not specific to AI-exposed industries, so they don't settle the question, but they mean the displacement case rests on one central finding rather than a pattern that shows up everywhere.

What to watch: job postings for AI-exposed occupations move earliest of any measure here, so that is the chart most likely to shift first if this reading is turning.
