The reading is displacement, and it is a weak one. It rests on deterioration that is actually present in the data, not on the absence of improvement: the number of people aged 25 to 54 in work has fallen by about 1.4 million over twelve months in the household survey, and the share of the unemployed who have been out of work more than six months has climbed 4 percentage points in a year to 27.3 percent. Those are things going wrong now, not things failing to get better.

Nothing changed since the last analysis. Not one panel's headline number moved. This is the same picture read again, and the honest description of it is a configuration that has parked in a displacement-shaped position rather than a new development.

What the exposed-versus-control jobs panel says. Industries where AI can do a meaningful share of the work — information, professional and business services, finance — are shrinking, down 1.27 percent in employment over the past year. Industries where the work is physical — construction, health care, hospitality, transport — are growing at 1.27 percent. Name the moving sides rather than the gap: one side is contracting in absolute terms, the other is expanding at a healthy clip. That combination is the strongest thing in favour of an AI-specific reading, because a general economic slowdown does not politely spare construction and hospitality while it hollows out office work. It is also worth saying that this gap is not widening. It was 2.61 points a quarter ago and is 2.54 points now, a slight narrowing, and at its worst in late 2023 it was more than twice as wide. The news here is persistence, not acceleration.

The hiring correction, weighed honestly. Exposed industries hired far too aggressively in 2021 and 2022 and have been unwinding it ever since. That explains why this gap opened and explains its 2023 extreme. Four years on it plausibly still explains much of the level. What it does not explain is why exposed employment is now negative in absolute terms while the control side grows, and why the gap has not travelled back toward its 2010s norm of roughly one point. A correction that has run four years and still leaves outright contraction is doing less work than it was doing in 2023. That residual is what the displacement reading is built on, and it is modest.

What argues hardest against the displacement reading. Three things, and none of them is decorative.

First, and most seriously, the freshest panel points the other way. Job postings are the earliest thing to move when hiring changes, and the postings data runs a month ahead of the employment data supporting this reading, so it cannot be waved off as stale. Postings for knowledge work sit 22.8 percent below their February 2020 level while postings for hands-on work sit 5.3 percent above theirs — a wide gap, but that gap has narrowed over the past six months, by 2.8 points. Much of the level is a 2022 interest-rate event that has simply stayed put and is not news. A narrowing spread is not what an accelerating displacement episode produces. It is not augmentation either: augmentation on this panel would look like knowledge-work postings climbing back toward their pre-COVID level, and being nearly a quarter below it is not that.

Second, productivity. Output per hour across the whole economy is growing at 2.8 percent a year, up from 2.5 percent, which is inside the range the late-1990s internet boom produced. That is what augmentation working would look like in the aggregate. Two things blunt it. It covers every worker in the country and cannot say anything about AI-exposed industries specifically. And it is five months old, three months older than the employment numbers carrying this reading, so it cannot yet reflect the recent turn. There is also a mechanical trap: output per hour rises when low-productivity hours are shed, so a displacement episode and an augmentation episode can print the same productivity number.

Third, people who change jobs are still doing well. Across the economy, job switchers are getting pay rises of 4.4 percent against 3.6 percent for people who stay put. When moving pays, people are usually moving toward opportunity rather than fleeing trouble. This is the single best argument that what is happening is reallocation rather than removal. Its limit is that it is an economy-wide median and cannot speak to exposed industries, and a collapse concentrated in a minority of workers is exactly what a median hides.

The contradiction inside the labour data, stated plainly. The employer survey says the economy added 57,000 jobs in June and unemployment is 4.2 percent. The household survey says prime-age employment fell over the year. These are different surveys of different things and neither refutes the other, but a reader trusting the prime-age number is owed the knowledge that the other main measure of employment is not corroborating it that month. The household survey is also the one with documented response-rate problems in this period, and almost the entire twelve-month fall in prime-age employment happened in a single quarter. If the employer survey is telling the truer story, this displacement reading is wrong.

Two further things that shape how the displacement should be characterised. Layoffs are running at 1.1 percent of employment and are flat — completely normal. Hiring is weak, at 3.3 percent, and quitting is weak too, at 1.9 percent. Weak hiring with normal layoffs is a frozen market, not a shedding one. Nobody is being pushed out; the cost falls on people trying to get in and on anyone who leaves. That fits the long-term-unemployment picture: exits are not being caught. But a sceptic can fairly say a frozen market is exactly what "displacement hasn't started, employers are just cautious" looks like from outside, and this data cannot fully rebut that.

Also, the stock of employment is still fine even as the flow has turned. The share of 25-to-54-year-olds in work remains about a quarter of a point above where it was in 2019. This is why the reading stays weak.

Alternatives considered and rejected. A general recession: rejected, because control industries are growing and their postings are above pre-pandemic levels, and layoffs are normal. A recession does not do that. An interest-rate-driven cut to white-collar hiring: real, and it explains the standing level of the postings gap, which opened with the 2022 tightening before current AI tools were widely deployed — but financial conditions are easing now, not tightening, so it explains a level rather than the current flow. Calling this month confounded would require naming a cause with a series in front of me showing it deteriorating, and there is none.

On the wage panel: pay in exposed industries is growing 4.63 percent against 3.53 percent in control industries, faster in the industries AI touches most. That looks like augmentation, but it cannot be used as evidence either way here, because exposed employment is falling. If entry-level hiring in those industries has stopped, the remaining workforce skews senior and the average wage rises with nobody getting a raise. Unresolved, and cited as support for nothing.

Two other numbers, recorded without being counted. AI adoption reached 21.7 percent of firms, up from 17.3 percent in November 2025, with much higher rates at large employers — this makes a displacement story possible but causes nothing on its own. And the share of national income going to workers has fallen for four straight quarters to 51.0 percent, the lowest in a series running back to 1947. Productivity rising while the labour share sets a record low means the aggregate gains are not reaching workers. It is an economy-wide number with forty years of pre-AI decline behind it, so it does not vote here, but it is the most striking figure on the board.

What would show this reading is wrong. If the prime-age employment rate's twelve-month change climbs back to roughly flat while the long-term-unemployed share falls back under 26 percent, the outflow from exposed work is being reabsorbed and the displacement reading fails. That series just moved half a point in three months, so retracing it inside a quarter is well within its demonstrated volatility.
