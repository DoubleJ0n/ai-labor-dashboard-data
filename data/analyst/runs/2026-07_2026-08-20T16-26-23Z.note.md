The reading stays on augmentation this month, but that word means displacement has not shown up in the data yet — it is not a finding that AI is visibly making exposed workers more valuable. That distinction matters because the positive case, that exposed workers are being paid more for genuinely more productive work, remains too tangled with other explanations to count as support on its own.

Almost nothing moved since last month. The one panel with fresh news is job postings: postings for knowledge-work occupations most exposed to AI, which have sat roughly 23% below their pre-2020 level for years, narrowed their gap with hands-on control occupations slightly, from 28.7 points behind to 27.9 points behind. That is a small, ambiguous wobble inside a spread that has been roughly flat since a 2022 contraction, not a turn. Every other panel — hiring, wages, productivity, adoption — is unchanged from a month ago.

The strongest thing keeping this call at augmentation is the prime-age employment picture: the share of 25-54 year-olds employed sits at 80.4%, unchanged over the past year and still above its pre-pandemic level, while a separate measure of hiring versus quits shows employers hiring faster than workers are quitting, with layoffs falling. If AI were actively pushing workers out of exposed industries and they were failing to land elsewhere, this is the kind of measure that would break down first, and it has not.

The strongest thing cutting against it is that AI-exposed industries — information, professional and business services, financial activities — have been shrinking for months, down 1.19% over the past year, while non-exposed industries — construction, leisure and hospitality, education and health — are growing, up 1.16%. That gap has sat past the point of concern for four straight monthly readings without closing. A gap that persists this long without narrowing is also consistent with a story where the reallocation already happened and simply is not reversing: a smaller exposed workforce that has settled at a new, lower level, rather than one still finding its way to new jobs. Separately, a broader measure of underemployment is rising and closing in on its own concerning level, and the faster three-month read on the prime-age employment ratio is falling even though the slower twelve-month figure this whole call rests on is still flat — a possible early crack the calmer number cannot show yet.

What to watch: the prime-age employment ratio on the reabsorption panel, specifically whether its three-month trend keeps falling into the twelve-month figure, since that slower number is the one this entire call is built on.
