The augmentation reading holds this month: the data still show industries most exposed to AI reallocating workers rather than shedding them en masse. That conclusion rests on the absence of deterioration in the numbers that would show displacement, not on positive proof that AI is making these workers more productive — worth stating plainly, since it is the whole basis for the call.

What's new since last month is minor and sits outside the panels that decide the call. The postings gap between AI-exposed and less-exposed occupations narrowed slightly, from -27.9 to -27.6, inside normal month-to-month noise. The worker share of national income fell further, now among the fastest declines in decades, but that is an economy-wide figure unrelated specifically to AI-exposed industries, and the update reflects newly available older data rather than a fresh one-month move. A benchmark of AI capability against human experts dipped slightly, which measures what AI can do, not what has happened to any worker's job.

The strongest support for the augmentation reading: the measure that tracks whether people leaving AI-exposed work land somewhere else shows no trouble. Employment among prime-working-age adults is exactly flat over the past year and sits above its pre-pandemic level. Layoffs are not elevated, and companies are still hiring faster than people are quitting. If a real wave of AI-driven displacement were underway, this is where it would show up first, and it isn't.

The strongest thing arguing against it: AI-exposed industries are still losing jobs while other industries keep growing. Employment in exposed industries fell 1.19% over the past year while the comparison group grew 1.16%, and that gap has held at roughly this size for four straight months without closing. It also just crossed a line the analysis treats as historically unusual, even though it improved slightly from the prior month. That is a real tension: a gap that is both newly abnormal and not accelerating is a plateau, not a resolution, and should not be read as reassuring. A broader underemployment measure sits close to the same unusual territory, though its recent trend is easing rather than worsening — ambiguous, not a clean confirmation either way. Separately, exposed-industry pay is growing faster than the comparison group's, 4.2% against 3.2%, which looks like good news for those workers, but exposed-industry employment is shrinking at the same time — when lower-paid workers leave a shrinking group first, the average pay of who remains rises for arithmetic reasons, with nobody actually getting a raise. That gap was not counted as evidence of augmentation.

What to watch: the employment rate for prime-working-age adults is the single number that would move first if this plateau tips into real displacement.
