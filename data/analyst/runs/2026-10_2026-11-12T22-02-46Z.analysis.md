The bottom line has not moved this month: the reading stays AUGMENTATION, meaning AI exposure in the economy is not showing up as net job destruction. It is important to be precise about what that means. This is not a finding that AI is making exposed workers more productive or better paid — the data to support that kind of claim either doesn't exist yet or can't be read cleanly this month. It is a finding that the labor market is not failing to re-absorb the people who do leave AI-exposed work, and that the industries most exposed to AI are not shedding jobs faster than a general slowdown would explain. The case rests on the absence of deterioration, not on positive evidence of AI making anyone better off.

What moved since last month: almost nothing. Of the twelve data panels on the dashboard, eleven are completely unchanged from a month ago. The one panel that did move is job postings: postings for AI-exposed occupations (software, analysis, writing-heavy work) widened their gap against postings for hands-on occupations (construction, hospitality, health and education work) for a sixth consecutive month, from 29.7 points below to 30.3 points below. Exposed-occupation postings now sit 21.6% below their own pre-pandemic level; hands-on-occupation postings sit 8.7% above theirs. This drift has been going on long enough, and far enough past the 2022 interest-rate shock that originally depressed postings economy-wide, that it is getting harder to write off as the residue of that older event. It genuinely looks like continued softening in demand for exposed-occupation labor at the margin.

The strongest evidence behind the AUGMENTATION reading is the employment data for prime-age workers (ages 25 to 54). Over the past year their employment-to-population ratio hasn't moved at all — flat — and it sits three-quarters of a point above its 2019 level, which was itself a strong labor market. If AI were pushing meaningful numbers of displaced workers into unemployment or out of the workforce, this is the chart where it would show up first at the aggregate level, and it is showing nothing. Alongside that, employment in AI-exposed industries is shrinking, down 1.35% over the past year, while employment in a set of industries AI barely touches (construction, leisure and hospitality, education and health) is growing a healthy 1.17%. That gap between exposed and untouched industries is real and has held for three months running. But a general economic downturn would hit the exposed side hard too, and it hasn't — the untouched side is still growing — which argues against "it's just a broad slowdown" as the explanation for the exposed-side weakness. One honest caveat: a chunk of that untouched-industry growth may be concentrated in health care specifically, which grows for demographic reasons unrelated to any AI effect, and we don't have the breakdown this month to confirm how much of the "control" strength is really construction and hospitality versus health care alone.

The strongest evidence against the reading, and the one that deserves to be read with full weight rather than softened, is that postings gap. It is the single most forward-looking indicator on the dashboard — job postings move before hiring and firing show up in the employment numbers — and it is the one panel moving in the opposite direction from the calm picture elsewhere, for six straight months now. A second, smaller but real counter-signal: the share of unemployed workers who have been out of work long-term rose 3.5 percentage points over the past year and that increase is accelerating, up 2.1 points in just the last three months. Taken together with job openings and job-quitting both drifting down slightly while layoffs stay low, this looks less like active firing and more like a market that has gone quiet — people who lose or leave a job are taking longer to land the next one, whether or not AI caused the original exit. That's consistent with, not contradictory to, a verdict of "no deterioration yet," but it means the labor market has less cushion than the flat headline number alone suggests.

Wages add little either way this month. The pay gap between AI-exposed and untouched industries collapsed from +0.66 points to +0.08, essentially flat, but since employment in exposed industries is shrinking, that number is just as easily explained by only the better-paid survivors remaining in the average as by any real wage story — this panel can't distinguish the two while exposed employment keeps falling. Economy-wide, individual workers who change jobs (a different, composition-immune measure, covering all workers, not just those in AI-exposed industries) are seeing a healthy pay bump when they switch, in line with the past decade's norm — that's at least a sign there's no broad "landed into a worse job" pattern happening across the economy generally, though it says nothing about exposed industries specifically. Economy-wide productivity growth, at 2.1%, has slipped two straight quarters below what you'd expect if AI were already paying off at scale, which argues against claiming augmentation is visibly working — there's no positive number to point to yet, only the absence of a negative one.

Several named alternative explanations were considered and set aside, not because they're wrong but because none of them explains the whole pattern. The 2022 rate-hike shock plausibly still depresses the level of job postings generally, but doesn't explain why the exposed-versus-control gap keeps widening years later. Temp-staffing and media-industry volatility (hit hard by the 2023 strikes) plausibly explain part of why exposed-industry jobs look weak, but that explanation has had three years to fade and the gap hasn't closed. None of these rises to a full competing-cause verdict; they're genuine caveats sitting underneath a directional reading that still points to AUGMENTATION, not reasons to abandon it.
