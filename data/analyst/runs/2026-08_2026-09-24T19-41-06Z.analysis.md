The reading is displacement, held over from last month, and confidence in it stays low. Nothing about the underlying labor-market data changed this time. The only figure that moved anywhere in this file is the share of firms reporting they use AI in any part of their business, which ticked up from 23.2% to 23.8%. That tells us AI use keeps spreading through firms — it does not tell us anything about jobs, because self-reported adoption has no link to any specific hiring or firing decision. Every other panel used in this reading carries the same numbers as a month ago.

Here is what those numbers say. Employment in AI-exposed industries has fallen 1.47% over the past year, while comparable industries with little AI exposure grew 1.4% over the same period. That gap has been widening for four straight readings, not appearing as a one-month blip, and it has now moved past the level flagged in advance as worth taking seriously. This is the strongest piece of evidence behind the displacement reading: something specific is happening to AI-exposed work, distinct from the rest of the economy.

Two things complicate that story enough to keep confidence low rather than moving it higher.

First, the earliest-moving indicator in this file disagrees. Job postings for AI-exposed occupations have not deteriorated further in six months — the six-month change sits essentially flat, close to zero — even while the slower-moving employment and reabsorption data keep softening. If AI-driven displacement were actively accelerating right now, the fastest-reacting series on this dashboard should be the first to show it. It isn't. That's a real disagreement between the fastest signal and the slower ones, not a footnote.

Second, the shape of the weakness looks like a freeze, not a purge. In the reabsorption data — which tracks how well people who lose or leave jobs are being reabsorbed into new ones — the share of long-term unemployed is rising and the prime-age employment rate has drifted down past its own deterioration marker over the past year. But layoffs are running below their normal level, and the flow of hires versus quits is essentially flat, not weak. Employers are not actively shedding workers they already employ. What's happening instead is that new entrants and people who lose jobs are having a harder time getting reabsorbed. That is consistent with either a cautious, uncertain hiring environment generally, or with AI substituting for entry-level and junior roles specifically — the data here cannot tell those two apart.

A genuine complication for the displacement reading, considered and not resolved: part of the weakness in AI-exposed industries sits in temp staffing and financial/real-estate categories, both classic early movers in any economic slowdown, AI-related or not. Against that, the industries used as the non-exposed comparison group are growing at a healthy 1.4% rate, and the broader economy is still adding jobs (payrolls up 162,000 in the most recent month, unemployment at 4.1%) — both arguing against a broad, ordinary downturn explanation. But the specific data needed to fully rule out a staffing-sector or rate-sensitive explanation isn't available here, so this stays an open question rather than a settled one.

On the augmentation side of the ledger, there's no clean positive signal to weigh against displacement. Pay in AI-exposed industries is growing faster than in the comparison industries, which would look like augmentation — except that exposed employment is simultaneously shrinking, and when the industry with faster pay growth is also losing workers, a shrinking pool of employees driving up the average could just as easily be composition (junior workers leaving first) as anyone getting genuinely more valuable. A separate check on economy-wide wages for people who actually stayed in the same job versus those who switched jobs shows no unusual gap between the two — unremarkable, and not able to confirm or rule out anything specific to exposed industries either way. Meanwhile, productivity growth for the whole economy came in at 2.2% for the most recent quarter, down from 2.9% and a touch below the pace that would suggest AI is delivering a broad efficiency dividend right now. None of this supports an augmentation story strongly enough to outweigh the displacement signal above.

Several other indicators in the data — measures of AI model capability on benchmark tasks, and model-versus-model leaderboards — were set aside entirely. They measure what AI systems can do in a test, not what employers are actually doing with workers, and none of them connects to an actual hiring or firing decision, so they carry no weight in this reading either way.

Net picture: a real, specific gap between AI-exposed and non-AI-exposed industries that has been widening for months, sitting alongside a labor market that looks frozen for new entrants rather than one where employers are cutting staff — while the fastest-moving indicator on the board, job postings, has stopped getting worse. That combination is why this stays displacement, and why it stays low confidence rather than moving higher.
