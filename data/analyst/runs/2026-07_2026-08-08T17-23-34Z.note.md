# Published note 2026-07 (run 2026-08-08T17:23:34Z)

Notification: Only the yield curve moved; no-displacement reading holds on absent damage, not gains.
Falsifier: The prime-age employment share moves first — if its 12-month change falls to -0.6 points or worse while control industries are still adding jobs at 0.8% a year or better, the outflow from AI-exposed work is no longer landing and this reading is wrong.

---

AI-exposed work is shrinking, but the people leaving it still appear to be landing in other jobs — reallocation rather than removal — and that reading rests on the absence of damage, not on any evidence that AI is making exposed workers more productive or better paid.

Nothing moved this month. Twelve of thirteen panels carry identical numbers to the last read. The single change is the gap between ten-year and two-year Treasury yields, from 0.4 to 0.5 points, which says the bond market is not pricing a recession and says nothing about AI.

What holds the reallocation reading up: people who lose work are still finding it. The share of 25-to-54-year-olds with a job is 80.4%, unchanged over twelve months and about half a point above its 2019 level. Of everyone unemployed, 25.5% have been out more than six months, up just 0.6 points on the year against a measure that normally swings by several points. Layoffs are running at 1.1% of employment a month and falling. Employers are not throwing people out; they are declining to replace those who leave. All three of those figures cover the whole economy, not AI-exposed workers specifically, which is a real limit on what they can prove.

What argues hard the other way, and it is a lot. Employment in AI-exposed industries — information, professional services, finance — is falling at 1.19% a year, an outright contraction, while construction, hospitality, education and health are adding jobs at 1.16%. Postings for knowledge work sit about 23% below their February 2020 level; postings for hands-on work are about 5% above theirs. Labour's share of all income in the economy is 51.0%, the lowest in a series running back to 1947, down four quarters straight and falling at a pace normally seen only in recessions — and unemployment is 4.1%, so this is not a recession. That reading is from March, five months old, so the strongest evidence against this verdict is older than the evidence for it.

Two more. The exposed-versus-control jobs gap was narrowest last November and has widened in every month since, across the same period in which firms reporting AI use rose from 17.3% to 21.5%; that is two aggregates moving together with no link between any firm's AI use and its own hiring, but it points the wrong way. And the employment share doing most of the work here covers ages 25 to 54 only. A frozen hiring market pushes the cost onto people trying to get in, and nobody under 25 appears in it. Nothing here answers that.

Pay in AI-exposed industries is growing faster than in control industries, 4.2% against 3.2%, which looks like the augmentation signature. Treat it carefully: when junior hiring stops, juniors drop out of the average and the average rises with nobody getting a raise. Nothing available separates that from a genuine pay story.

Watch the prime-age employment share. It is flat over twelve months but has drifted down 0.3 points over the last three, and that is where a turn would show first.
