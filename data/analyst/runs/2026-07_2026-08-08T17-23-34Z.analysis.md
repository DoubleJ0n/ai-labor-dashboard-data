The reading this month is that AI-exposed work is shrinking but the people leaving it are still landing in jobs — reallocation rather than removal. That reading rests almost entirely on the absence of damage, not on evidence that AI is making exposed workers more productive or better paid. It is held with low confidence, and the case against it is substantial enough that it takes up as much room below as the case for.

WHAT ACTUALLY CHANGED

Almost nothing. Since the last read, twelve of the thirteen panels carry byte-identical numbers. The single move is in bond yields: the gap between ten-year and two-year Treasuries went from 0.4 to 0.5 points. That gap being positive rather than negative means the bond market is not pricing an ordinary recession. It says nothing whatever about AI, and it does not touch any employment series. Everything else here is yesterday's picture, re-read.

THE CASE FOR REALLOCATION

Three things say people are still landing.

The share of 25-to-54-year-olds with a job is 80.4%, which is exactly where it was twelve months ago and about half a point above where it stood in 2019. If a large group of workers were being pushed out of employment altogether, this is where it would show, and it has not moved.

Of everyone unemployed, 25.5% have been out of work more than six months. That is up 0.6 points over the year, against a measure that routinely swings by several points. When people who lose jobs cannot find new ones, this figure climbs hard. It has barely twitched.

Layoffs and discharges are running at 1.1% of employment a month, slightly lower than a year ago. Hiring is at 3.4% and quits at 2.0%. Read together, that is a frozen market rather than a shrinking one: employers are not throwing people out, they are declining to replace the ones who leave. This comes from a survey of employers, a genuinely separate instrument from the household survey that produces the other two figures, and it agrees with them.

All three of these numbers cover the whole economy, every industry and both sexes and all sectors — not AI-exposed workers specifically. That is an important limit. They can tell you that the economy as a whole is absorbing people. They cannot tell you that the specific people leaving information, professional services and finance are the ones being absorbed.

One economy-wide figure does speak to how movers fare: people who changed employers over the past year got pay rises of 4.4%, against 3.6% for people who stayed put. That premium is normal by pre-2020 standards, and because it follows the same individuals a year apart, it cannot be manufactured by changes in who is in the sample. Job-changing still pays. That is the closest thing here to direct evidence that people who move land well.

THE CASE AGAINST, AT FULL STRENGTH

Employment in AI-exposed industries — information, professional and business services, finance — is falling at 1.19% a year. That is an outright contraction, not slower growth. Meanwhile the control industries — construction, leisure and hospitality, education and health — are adding jobs at 1.16% a year. The weakness is specific, not general.

Advertised demand tells the same story on a different cut and from a different source. Postings for knowledge work sit about 23% below their February 2020 level. Postings for hands-on work sit about 5% above theirs. A knowledge-work hiring market a fifth smaller than before the pandemic, for years running, is a standing forecast of continued weakness in exposed hiring.

Labour's share of all income generated in the economy is 51.0%, which is the lowest reading in a series that begins in 1947 — below the previous record set only last December. It has fallen four quarters running, and the fall over the past year is among the fastest tenth on record. Falls this fast normally happen in recessions, and we are not in one: unemployment is 4.1% and payrolls are roughly flat. A record low in labour's share, falling at recession speed outside a recession, is a genuine finding and I am not going to bury it. Two caveats belong with it, and neither dissolves it. This share has drifted down for forty years for reasons that long predate AI. And this reading is from March 2026, five months old — the newest evidence arguing against the reallocation reading is older than the evidence supporting it, which means it cannot yet reflect any recent turn either way.

The exposed-versus-control jobs gap was at its narrowest in November of last year and has been wider in every month since, across the same nine months in which the share of firms reporting AI use rose from 17.3% to 21.5%. Two aggregates moving together prove nothing — the adoption survey asks whether a tool is present somewhere in the business, not whether any role was made redundant, and nothing links a particular firm's "yes" to that firm's hiring — but it is the single strongest thing in this data pointing the other way, and it is the main reason confidence is low.

The employment share that carries most of the weight here covers ages 25 to 54, on purpose, to keep retirements out. That also makes it blind to people trying to enter the workforce. And a frozen market — few layoffs, unfilled vacancies — is exactly the shape that pushes the whole cost onto entrants. If AI is eating junior knowledge work, this data would show a flat prime-age employment share, low layoffs and depressed knowledge-work postings, which is precisely what it does show. There is no panel here that answers this, and it is the strongest single objection to the reallocation reading.

Finally, the prime-age employment share has been drifting down over the last three months, by 0.3 points, even as the twelve-month change sits at zero. Three months on a noisy monthly series is a hypothesis about a turn, not a turn. But it is the configuration that would drag the twelve-month figure down if it persists, and it is why the condition that would overturn this reading is set on that exact number.

THE PAY PANEL, AND WHY IT IS NOT EVIDENCE

Average pay in AI-exposed industries is growing at 4.22% a year against 3.21% in the control industries. On its face that is the augmentation signature: exposed workers getting more valuable, not less. I am not treating it as evidence, and the reason is on the same panel. Exposed employment is falling at 1.19% a year, and the postings data says entry-level hiring in those industries has largely stopped. When juniors stop being hired, they drop out of the average and the average rises without anybody getting a raise. The control side has the mirror problem: it contains leisure and hospitality, low-paid and growing, which drags its average down mechanically. A real pay story and a pure composition artefact predict the identical number, and nothing available separates them.

ALTERNATIVES CONSIDERED AND REJECTED

Interest rates. Borrowing costs are genuinely restrictive — the ten-year yield is about 2.4% after inflation — and much of the knowledge-work postings collapse dates to 2022, when the tightening cycle began, not to any model release. But construction, the most rate-sensitive industry in the economy, sits in the control group, and construction and its peers are growing at 1.16% a year with hands-on postings above their pre-pandemic level. Rates plausibly explain part of the exposed side, particularly real estate and insurance. They cannot explain a control side this healthy. So rates are a caveat on attribution, not the explanation.

A general downturn. Same test, same answer: a general contraction pulls both sides down, and only one side is down. July payrolls did fall by 23,000, which is soft, but that is one preliminary month covering the whole economy and it does not distinguish exposed from control work.

Federal contracting and temporary help. Professional and business services contains both, and both turn early and hard for reasons with no AI in them. This is plausible and, with the data at hand, untestable — there is no series that separates them out. It is a standing weakness in using industries as a proxy for AI exposure, and it is logged as one rather than promoted to an explanation.

The 2023 writers' and actors' strikes. The exposed-versus-control gap was at its most extreme in late 2023, exactly when a labour dispute in motion pictures was suppressing employment in the information sector. That matters for reading the history: the peak of this series is partly a strike, and the gap today is roughly a third as extreme as that peak.

WHAT THE PRODUCTIVITY DATA SAYS, WHICH IS NOTHING HELPFUL

Output per hour is growing at 2.2%, down from 2.9%. That is better than the 2010s average of about 1.3%, but well short of the 1990s internet-boom pace and short of anything that would count as a break in trend. If AI were delivering large measurable gains, this is where they would surface, and they have not. Worse, this measure could not tell you which story it supported if it did move: rising output per hour with falling hours in exposed industries is the arithmetic of replacement just as much as of augmentation. It is a check that failed to find positive evidence for the reallocation reading, and I am counting it that way rather than dressing it up.

TWO GAPS IN THE DATA WORTH KNOWING ABOUT

The hiring and quits figures are published as current levels with a change in their difference, but without last year's levels for each. That means it is impossible to say whether the small improvement in hiring-minus-quitting came from more hiring or from fewer people daring to quit. Those are opposite stories — a market absorbing people versus workers too frightened to move — and both read as improvement on this measure. That is an unresolved question inside a panel this reading leans on.

And the household employment figures step through an annual population re-basing each January, which puts artificial jumps in the underlying counts of employed people and of the population. The employment share itself is safe because both parts of the ratio are re-based together, which is why this analysis quotes the share and not the underlying counts.

WHAT WOULD SETTLE IT

If the share of 25-to-54-year-olds in work falls by 0.6 points or more over twelve months while the control industries are still adding jobs at 0.8% a year or better, the outflow from AI-exposed work has stopped landing, and the reallocation reading published here is wrong. Both halves matter: a general recession would drag the control industries down too, and a general recession is a different thing from AI displacement. That employment share moved by 0.8 points in a single monthly print earlier this year, so a move of this size in a quarter is well within what the series does.
