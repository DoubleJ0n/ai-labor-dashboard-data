The reading is augmentation, not displacement, and it rests mainly on the absence of deterioration rather than on direct proof that AI is helping these workers do more. That distinction matters: nothing in this month's data shows AI-exposed workers landing in better jobs or earning more for genuine productivity reasons. What it shows is that the people leaving AI-exposed industries are not piling up unemployed, and that is a meaningfully different, weaker claim than "augmentation is working."

Nothing moved since the prior run two days earlier. Every panel carries the same values it had on 2026-10-06, so this update confirms the same position for a second reading rather than adding new evidence either way.

The core case for augmentation sits in two places. First, employment in AI-exposed industries is falling 1.6% year over year while employment in comparable non-exposed control industries is rising 1.19% year over year — a gap of 2.79 points, wider than anything in the clean pre-2020 record for this series. That is a real, specific signal that something distinguishes AI-exposed work from the rest of the economy. Second, and this is the panel built to answer whether that outflow is landing anywhere: the prime-age (25-54) employment-population ratio, the best available gauge of whether displaced workers are finding other jobs, is flat over the past twelve months and sits slightly above its 2019 level. Of four supporting readouts in that same panel, two are improving (the gap between hires and quits is widening in hiring's favor, and the layoff rate is falling) and one is mildly improving (the gap between the underemployed and the officially unemployed is narrowing). Control-industry job growth itself, at 1.19% year over year, is not a recessionary pace. Put together: workers are leaving AI-exposed industries in unusual numbers, but the broader labor market is absorbing them rather than stranding them. That is what augmentation (or at least "not mass displacement") looks like from this kind of data.

The strongest fact cutting against that reading is the one point of this reabsorption picture that is not improving, paired with a standing gap elsewhere that has never closed. Long-term unemployment's share of all unemployment has risen 3.5 points over the past year, the one secondary indicator moving the wrong way, even as the headline ratio holds flat. And separately, job postings in AI-exposed occupations remain 21.8% below their February 2020 level, while postings in non-exposed control occupations sit 8.2% above theirs — a 30-point gap that has barely moved in six months, holding steady even as the share of firms reporting AI use rose from 17.3% to 23.8% over the same stretch. A gap that size, sitting still rather than closing, in exactly the occupations AI is supposed to affect, is consistent with a reading where demand for this kind of knowledge work has been structurally depressed and simply has not reversed. That reading points toward displacement, not augmentation, and postings are the kind of indicator that would move first if the picture here is turning. The reason this does not flip the call is that the gap is not widening through the exact window AI adoption has accelerated — it opened earlier, in the 2022 interest-rate tightening, and has been flat since — but a flat, unclosed gap is itself a finding, not a reassurance, and it is the main reason this call carries low rather than medium confidence.

One number that looks like strong augmentation evidence on its face needs a caveat. Pay in AI-exposed industries is growing faster than in control industries, 4.12% against 3.40% year over year. Read alone, that looks like AI-touched work is becoming more valuable. But exposed-industry employment is shrinking at the same time, which means this could equally be a survivorship effect — senior staff staying while junior staff leave, raising the average pay of who's left without anyone getting an actual raise — rather than genuine wage gains from augmentation. A separate, economy-wide tracker of same-individual pay growth (not specific to AI-exposed industries) shows job switchers earning a 0.8-point premium over people who stay put, in line with its normal historical range, which does not resolve the question either way. This wage gap is therefore not being counted as confirming evidence for augmentation.

Two further checks round out the picture, both economy-wide rather than specific to AI-exposed work, and both logged here for completeness rather than counted toward the verdict directly. Labor productivity across the whole economy slowed to 2.2% from 2.9% the prior quarter, which cuts against any story of AI visibly accelerating output per worker. And the yield curve is not inverted, meaning markets are not currently pricing a recession, which is a backdrop fact rather than evidence about AI specifically.

What would overturn this reading: the prime-age employment ratio's 12-month change falling to -0.6 points or worse while control-industry job growth still holds up at 0.5% or better year over year — a combination that would mean the outflow from AI-exposed industries has stopped landing elsewhere even though the rest of the economy is fine, which is the specific signature of AI-driven displacement rather than a general slowdown.
