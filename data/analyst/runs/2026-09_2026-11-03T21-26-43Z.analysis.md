The reading this month is DISPLACEMENT: AI-exposed industries are shrinking while industries AI has barely touched keep growing, and the broader labor market shows no clear sign of absorbing the people leaving those shrinking industries. This call is held with low confidence, for a reason explained below that matters as much as the verdict itself.

What changed since last month is almost nothing in the data. The one series that moved was the macro-regime check (the gap between long- and short-term government borrowing costs), which ticked from 0.4 to 0.3 — still comfortably in territory that signals no general economic slowdown. Every other panel printed the same figures as a month ago: exposed-industry jobs still down 1.6% over the year against control industries up 1.19%; exposed-industry pay still running ahead of control-industry pay; job postings for knowledge work still roughly 22 points below their pre-pandemic level while postings for hands-on work sit about 8 points above theirs; the prime-age employment rate still flat over the past year. The verdict moved from AUGMENTATION to DISPLACEMENT on essentially the same numbers that produced AUGMENTATION last time. That is worth sitting with: nothing deteriorated this month, nothing new broke. What changed is which side of a close argument carried more weight, and the argument is close enough that it has swung between these two readings several times in the past few months without the underlying employment picture actually shifting much.

The strongest fact behind DISPLACEMENT is that the contraction in AI-exposed work is specific, not general, and it is confirmed by two independent measurements. Employment in AI-exposed industries is falling at 1.6% a year while employment in non-exposed control industries is growing at 1.19% a year — industries that face AI are shrinking next to industries that largely don't, at the same time, in the same economy. A second, independent dataset built from job postings rather than payroll counts tells a similar story: postings for knowledge work sit about 22 points below where they stood before the pandemic, while postings for hands-on, harder-to-automate work sit about 8 points above their own pre-pandemic level. Two different data sources, cut two different ways, both show the exposed side falling while the unexposed side rises. A broad recession would be expected to hit both sides together; it isn't. That combination is why this is read as displacement rather than a general downturn.

There is a real catch in that postings comparison, though, and it needs to be stated plainly rather than softened. The 22-point gap between exposed and non-exposed postings opened in 2022, during the interest-rate-driven slowdown in hiring, and it has barely moved in the past six months. That means most of this gap is a standing wound from a 2022 event, not something currently getting worse. It corroborates that exposed work is weaker than unexposed work right now, but it should not be read as an accelerating, ongoing AI effect — it's closer to a scar that stopped healing three years ago than to fresh damage.

The strongest fact against DISPLACEMENT, and the one that keeps this call at low confidence, is that the panel built specifically to show whether displaced workers are failing to land elsewhere is not showing failure. The share of prime-age adults who are employed — the cleanest available measure of whether people who lose exposed-industry jobs are being reabsorbed into other work — has not moved at all over the past year. It sits flat, well short of the decline that would mark workers being pushed out of the labor market altogether. More tellingly, the fastest-moving cut of this same measure, covering just the last three months, is improving rather than worsening. If AI-driven job losses were starting to outpace the economy's ability to reabsorb those workers, this is the chart that would show it first, and right now it is moving the wrong way for that story.

Two other figures deserve a flag rather than being taken at face value. Pay in AI-exposed industries is rising faster than pay in control industries, 4.12% against 3.4%. On its surface that looks like good news for the workers who remain — but exposed-industry employment is shrinking, and when a shrinking workforce loses its newer, lower-paid workers first, the remaining average pay rises mechanically even if no one actually got a raise. A separate, economy-wide measure of job-switcher pay (not split by exposed versus non-exposed industries) shows switching jobs is currently paying better than normal across the whole economy — which says something about the general wage climate but, because it isn't cut by AI exposure, cannot confirm or rule out whether the exposed-industry pay gap is real or just this compositional effect. The honest reading is that this pay data is currently uninformative rather than supportive of either side.

Economy-wide productivity growth, a general check on whether AI gains are showing up in aggregate output, slowed from 2.9% to 2.2% over the year to mid-2026, below the range that would mark a productivity boom. This covers the whole economy, not AI-exposed workers specifically, and may simply reflect gains that haven't shown up yet — it is used here only to decline calling this an augmentation success story, not as direct evidence of displacement. Separately, the worker share of national income has slipped a point over the past year, among the faster declines on record, but this is an economy-wide, decades-long trend predating AI, and past recessions have produced faster drops than this one — it's background context, not a lever on the verdict. AI adoption among employers continues to climb, from about 17% to about 24% of firms over the measured period, concentrated more heavily in larger employers than small ones, which rules out the objection that there isn't enough real AI deployment yet to be causing anything — it doesn't by itself prove displacement is happening, just that the opportunity for it exists.

Finally, a macro check: the gap between long- and short-term borrowing costs remains in normal, non-inverted territory, and control industries are themselves healthy and growing. Both facts argue against a general economic downturn as the explanation for the weakness concentrated in AI-exposed industries. No other named cause in the data — a slowdown in voluntary job-switching tied to worker caution has been raised elsewhere as a partial explanation for one secondary reading, but it does not explain why exposed-industry headcount itself is contracting while unexposed-industry headcount is not.

Put together: this is a real, two-source-confirmed contraction in AI-exposed work sitting next to a reabsorption measure that has not deteriorated and, on its most current reading, is improving. That is a thin case for displacement, not a strong one, and it has flipped against augmentation on the same facts that supported augmentation a month ago. The panel to watch is the prime-age employment rate; if its 12-month change starts falling, that would show the gap between exposed and unexposed work is starting to translate into people failing to find other work, not just moving between jobs.
