The bottom line: this month's reading is DISPLACEMENT, the same call made last time, held at low confidence. Nothing in the underlying data moved between the last review and this one — every series is still showing the same month's numbers (August 2026 for the jobs and reabsorption data, June 2026 for productivity, September 2026 for job postings). That absence of movement is itself the news: no panel's headline figure changed, so the case for and against this verdict is exactly what it was last time, and it is worth setting out in full for anyone reading it fresh.

The case for displacement rests mainly on one panel. Employment in AI-exposed industries — the occupational categories most plausibly affected by AI tools — fell 1.47% over the past year, while employment in a comparison group of industries with little AI exposure (construction, hospitality, health care, education) grew 1.4% over the same period. That gap has been widening for four consecutive monthly readings, not just this one, and this month it crossed the level the dashboard treats as a clear warning rather than routine noise. That is a real, specific, and building signal that something is happening to hiring in exposed industries that is not happening in the rest of the economy.

But there is an important limit on what that one panel can tell you, and it matters enough to state plainly: a clean handoff, where workers leave shrinking exposed industries and are smoothly re-employed elsewhere, would produce exactly the same reading as workers being displaced with nowhere to go. The jobs panel alone cannot tell the two apart. To distinguish them you need to look at whether the rest of the labor market is absorbing the people leaving those industries, and that picture is decidedly mixed.

The prime-age employment rate — the share of 25-to-54-year-olds working, a broad measure of whether the labor market is absorbing people — is down 0.3 points over the past year. That is enough to count as a genuine soft patch by the dashboard's own standard, and it is the second leg of the case for displacement. But look at what usually accompanies real displacement, and most of it isn't there. Layoffs across the economy are not rising, they are falling. The rate at which people are hired relative to how many quit their jobs is flat, not the frozen, shedding pattern you'd expect if employers were cutting staff broadly. The share of the unemployed who have been out of work long-term has ticked up only mildly. Three of the four supporting indicators that would normally travel alongside a real displacement wave are calm or improving, not worsening. That is the strongest thing arguing against this verdict, and it is not a minor footnote: it is why confidence here is rated low rather than medium.

There is a second point working against the displacement read. The earliest-moving indicator on the dashboard — the gap between job postings for knowledge work and job postings for hands-on work — actually narrowed slightly this month rather than widening further. Postings for knowledge work remain deeply depressed against their pre-pandemic level (down about 22%) while postings for hands-on work are above theirs (up about 6%), but the direction of change over the past six months is toward those two lines coming closer together, not further apart. If displacement were building, this is the panel that should move first and furthest, and instead it drifted the other way.

One more thread deserves care rather than a quick read. Pay in AI-exposed industries is growing faster than in the comparison industries, 4.0% against 3.2% over the past year. Taken alone that looks like evidence against displacement — workers left in exposed industries are earning more, not less. But exposed-industry employment is shrinking at the same time, and when a workforce is shrinking, the people who leave first tend to be the more junior, lower-paid ones, which mechanically pulls the average pay of who's left upward even if no individual got a raise. A separate tracker that follows the same individuals over time, rather than an industry average, shows pay gains close to their historical norm and essentially unchanged from last month. That tracker not showing anything unusual is why this note treats the wage gap as inconclusive rather than as support for either side.

Two further pieces of context, and neither should be mixed in with the exposed-industry findings above, because they describe the whole economy rather than the industries AI is most likely to touch. Economy-wide productivity growth slowed to 2.2% from 2.9%, now below the range that would mark AI translating into visible economy-wide efficiency gains — a sign that augmentation is not yet showing up as a broad payoff, not a sign of displacement. And the broader economy is still expanding on its own terms: the unemployment rate sits at 4.1%, payrolls grew, and the bond market is not pricing a recession, which argues against a general downturn explaining the exposed-industry weakness. Separately, the share of AI-using firms rose from 17.3% to 23.2% over the past year, concentrated more heavily among large employers — useful background on how far AI deployment has spread, but it says nothing by itself about whether any specific job was gained or lost.

Alternative explanations were considered and set aside without being fully ruled out. A broad cyclical slowdown is contradicted by the health of the comparison industries and the shape of the bond market. A narrower explanation — that interest-rate-sensitive sectors folded into the "exposed" category, such as financial services and real estate, are dragging the numbers down for reasons unrelated to AI — cannot be ruled out with the data on hand, since no series in this review isolates that effect cleanly. It remains a live uncertainty rather than a resolved one.

Taken together: a real and building industry-specific gap between exposed and comparison employment, sitting against a labor market whose broader absorption signals are still mostly calm. That combination is why the verdict leans toward displacement without being confident about it.
