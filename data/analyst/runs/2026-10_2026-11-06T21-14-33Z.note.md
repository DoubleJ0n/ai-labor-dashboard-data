---

The verdict stays AUGMENTATION this month, and it is worth being plain about what that rests on: the absence of AI-exposed workers being pushed out of the labor market and failing to find other work, not any positive sign that AI is making workers more productive or better paid. No panel this month shows that second thing happening.

Two numbers moved since last month, in opposite directions. The gap between job growth in AI-exposed industries and job growth in industries AI barely touches (construction, leisure, education and health) narrowed from 2.79 percentage points to 2.52 points — still weak, but easing rather than widening, for the first time in three months. Separately, the pay gap between the same two groups nearly disappeared: exposed-industry pay had been growing 0.66 points faster than control-industry pay; the two are now within 0.08 points of each other. That convergence is hard to read cleanly, because employment in exposed industries is also shrinking (down 1.35% over the year), so fewer, different workers remaining in the data could produce the same number as a genuine pay shift. Economy-wide productivity growth, the number that would show augmentation actually working, ticked down to 2.1% from 2.3% and remains below the pace seen in past technology-driven productivity booms — a second straight quarterly decline, not an improvement.

The strongest evidence behind the verdict: the prime-age (25-54) employment rate was flat over the past year and sits above its pre-pandemic level, and layoffs are running below normal rather than above it. That is a frozen, cautious hiring market — hard on people trying to get in — rather than one where incumbent workers are being actively cut loose. That distinction is what keeps this reading AUGMENTATION rather than DISPLACEMENT.

The strongest evidence against it: job postings for AI-exposed occupations sit 21.6% below their pre-pandemic level, against postings for less-exposed occupations running 8.1% above theirs. That roughly 30-point gap has been completely flat for six months — not worsening, but not healing either. It is the fastest-moving indicator here, and it is not telling a reassuring story; it is showing a standing weakness in knowledge-work hiring that four years have not repaired. A related wrinkle worth naming directly: the share of unemployed people out of work long-term rose faster over the last three months than its own twelve-month trend would suggest, even though the headline employment number the verdict depends on hasn't moved yet.

What to watch: the prime-age employment rate inside the reabsorption data, because its own short-term unemployment-duration reading is already wobbling ahead of it.
