# Published note 2026-06 (run 2026-07-30T04:19:57Z)

Notification: Displacement, held weakly: prime-age employment rate down 0.5 points in a year.
Falsifier: The prime-age employment-rate chart moves first — if its 12-month change climbs back to -0.1 or better by late October while the long-term-unemployed share falls to 26% or under, the market absorbed the outflow and this displacement reading was wrong.

---

The reading is displacement, and it is held weakly.

It rests on something actually getting worse rather than on the absence of good news. The share of people aged 25 to 54 who are working has fallen half a percentage point over the past year, and it fell because roughly 1.44 million fewer prime-age people are employed, not because the population grew. Alongside that, employment in AI-exposed industries — information, professional and business services, finance — is down 1.27% over the year, while employment in industries AI has barely touched is up 1.27%. The exposed side is shrinking outright, not merely growing slowly.

Two limits on that, stated up front. The industry comparison covers workers in particular sectors; the employment-rate figure covers every prime-age adult in the country. Nothing here shows the people who stopped working are the ones who left exposed industries — someone who moved from a software firm to a hospital counts as a loss on one side and a gain on the other, and never touches the employment rate at all. And the entire annual decline in the employment rate happened in one quarter, between March and June. That could be survey noise that reverses. It also sits entirely after January's annual population-baseline revision, so that particular bookkeeping artifact cannot be causing the recent drop, though it may well be inflating the twelve-month figure. The level is still high: at 80.2%, the prime-age employment rate remains about a quarter point above 2019.

Nobody is being pushed out. Layoffs are at 1.1% of employment and have not moved in a year, which is normal. Hiring and quitting are both weak. That is a frozen market, not a shedding one: incumbents are safe, and the cost falls on people trying to get in or get back in. Consistent with that, the share of unemployed people who have been out of work more than six months has risen to 27.3% from 23.3% a year ago — exits are increasingly not being caught. One caution about the hiring numbers: the net difference between hiring and quitting improved slightly, but the published figures do not say which side moved. Since both rates are historically low, the likeliest reading is that quitting fell faster than hiring — workers not daring to move — which is the opposite of what the net figure superficially suggests. That is an inference, not a measurement.

The strongest thing arguing against this reading is job postings, and it is the freshest evidence available, a month ahead of everything else. Advertised openings for knowledge work sit about 23% below their February 2020 level while openings for hands-on work sit about 4% above theirs — but almost all of that hole dates from the 2022 interest-rate shock and is old news. What is current is the direction: the gap has narrowed by about four points over six months and narrowed again this month. Postings normally move before employment does, so the series that should lead is pointing the other way, and it cannot be waved off as stale. What keeps it from overturning the verdict is that four points off a twenty-three point hole is small, and postings are advertised intentions rather than filled jobs.

Pay is genuinely ambiguous and is not counted for either side. Wages in exposed industries are growing 4.63% a year against 3.53% in control industries, and that gap widened four-tenths of a point in a single month — backwards from what replacement would look like. But it cannot be read at face value while exposed employment is contracting: if junior hiring has stopped, the remaining workforce skews senior and the average rises with nobody getting a raise. Economy-wide, job-changers are still doing better than job-stayers, 4.4% against 3.6%, which says movers are landing well, but that measure covers the whole economy and cannot speak to exposed industries. Productivity growth of 2.8%, up from 2.5%, is what real gains look like — though output per hour also rises when hours fall, and exposed-industry hours are falling.

The best competing explanation is the 2021-22 over-hiring unwind. Technology and professional-services firms hired far too aggressively then and have been correcting since; the exposed-versus-control employment gap was considerably worse in late 2023 than now. That correction explains most of the level of today's gap. What it explains badly is why, four years after the surge and two years past the worst of the correction, the gap has not returned toward normal but has parked in place across four straight readings, with the exposed side contracting in absolute terms. A one-off correction should have finished unwinding. That non-closing residual is the actual signal here, and it is thin.

The interest-rate story — high rates freezing hiring in rate-sensitive white-collar work, which is largely the same set of industries labelled exposed — is not fully excluded. Against it: the bond market is not pricing a downturn, and the control industries are healthy on both employment and postings, which a general shock would not permit. For it: the postings collapse dates to the 2022 tightening, not to any AI release.

Watch the prime-age employment rate chart. If it turns back up over the next few months, the market absorbed the outflow and this was a hiring wobble rather than displacement.
